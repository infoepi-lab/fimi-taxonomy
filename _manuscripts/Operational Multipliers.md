# Overview {#overview}

**Operational Multipliers** are not message creators—they are force expanders. They aim to increase the scale, persistence, and perceived legitimacy of influence operations without altering the core narrative. They do not produce propaganda but make it louder, more persistent, and harder to counter. These mechanisms act across digital, institutional, and commercial environments to simulate popularity, engineer credibility, and embed influence into the informational terrain.

Unlike Legitimation, which draws power from social or institutional prestige, or Obfuscation, which hides the origin of influence, Operational Multipliers extend the effects of influence horizontally and vertically. They make a narrative feel urgent, ubiquitous, and endorsed—not through trust in the source, but through repetition, reach, and engineered resonance.

These tools are modular, reusable, and often deniable. Many are third-party actors, including commercial entities, freelance influencers, criminal enablers, and loosely aligned civil groups. Some are automated, others organic but coordinated. Their power is not in the argument, but in the architecture. They are designed to alter the informational environment itself.

Operational Multipliers are actors and mechanisms that **amplify the impact** of influence operations without producing the original message. Their role is not to create content but to **increase its visibility, credibility, and persistence**, making propaganda appear popular, trustworthy, and widely accepted. These tools operate across digital, institutional, and commercial environments, enhancing the reach and durability of core narratives.

Key types of operational multipliers include:

* **Commercial Malign Influence**: Private firms and digital mercenaries who offer information manipulation as a service, commodifying influence operations and enabling deniability.  
* **Data Grooming and Poisoning**: Strategic seeding of biased or false content into AI training datasets to subtly shape future machine outputs and limit epistemic boundaries.  
* **Influencer Networks**: Synchronized online personas who simulate consensus and credibility by mutually reinforcing aligned narratives.  
* **Mil-Bloggers**: Nationalist or veteran commentators who appear independent but validate regime goals while masking them as grassroots critique.  
* **Organized Criminal Activity**: Embedded criminal groups that execute influence functions covertly, often serving as proxies for state operations.  
* **Social Media Manipulation**: Botnets, trolls, and algorithm gaming used to fabricate engagement, suppress dissent, and engineer virality.

* **State-Aligned Social Groups**: Foreign-facing activist-style organizations that co-opt local grievances to disseminate regime-aligned narratives under the guise of solidarity.  
* **State-Content Aggregators**: Alternative media platforms that normalize and embed state propaganda within broader ideological media diets.  
* **State-Media Partnerships**: Formal collaborations between authoritarian regimes and foreign media outlets to lend legitimacy to state narratives.  
* **Train-the-Trainers Programs**: Long-term influence structures that cultivate local actors to propagate aligned messaging organically within their communities.

These multipliers work horizontally (spreading messages across networks) and vertically (embedding them into trusted institutions or systems). Their power lies in architecture, not authorship, making influence campaigns harder to detect, challenge, or reverse. They form the backbone of modern information warfare by turning isolated signals into full-spectrum cognitive environments.

# Varieties {#varieties}

## Commercial Malign Influence {#commercial-malign-influence}

Information manipulation has become a global service industry [\[1–4\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/QAY95+yKW0Q+50OVw+yIdSR). Commercial digital mercenaries represent the industrialization of information manipulation. These private-sector actors—typically PR firms, marketing agencies, freelance strategists, and cyber consultancies—offer bespoke information manipulation services for a price. Their emergence marks a pivotal evolution in the influence ecosystem: the decoupling of strategic information manipulation from state infrastructure.

These firms do not operate out of ideological loyalty. They are not organs of state propaganda, nor are they necessarily aligned with the geopolitical interests of their clients. They are service providers, operating under the logic of commercial consultancy. Their product is distortion: a menu of options including bot deployment, persona fabrication, engagement inflation, search engine manipulation, microtargeted content, troll-for-hire programs, and even adversarial takedown campaigns.

This commodification has turned what was once the exclusive domain of intelligence agencies into a globally accessible toolkit. Whether the client is a government, an oligarch, a political party, a corporation, or a foreign intelligence proxy, these actors offer off-the-shelf solutions to generate controversy, suppress dissent, or manufacture synthetic consensus . Influence is scalable, modular, and exportable.

Journalists and researchers, including the Oxford Internet Institute and the Mozilla Foundation, have investigated and found that these mercenaries operate transnationally [\[3,5\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/dwAZo+50OVw). Political elites in fragile democracies or conflict-prone regions often hire them. Regrettably, it is often people from democratic states who provide these services to elites in authoritarian countries [\[6,7\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/wPAJe+nRDLw). 

Their tactics are adaptive and increasingly AI-assisted. Some efforts seed content into AI training datasets to bias future generative model outputs [\[8\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/GNKWH). Others offer multi-platform packages that amplify aligned narratives and suppress opposition through content flagging, harassment swarms, or astroturf campaigns that simulate public outrage. Campaigns are often localized, appropriating native idioms, exploiting cultural divisions, and mimicking the style of authentic social movements to evade detection.

When unethical influence is no longer state-sponsored but state-commissioned, delivered through commercial intermediaries who may subcontract further, tracing its origin becomes legally and analytically complex. Plausible deniability becomes not a strategic layer but a structural feature.

Commercial digital mercenaries are operational multipliers par excellence. They do not create the underlying motive for influence but dramatically enhance its effect. They enable less capable actors to wage sophisticated campaigns, lower the technical and legal barriers to entry, and globalize access to information manipulation capabilities.

The consequence is a new informational arms trade. Strategic deception is now a service economy. Disinformation is available at competitive rates, detached from ideology, and increasingly protected by the legal ambiguity of “communications” or “engagement strategy.” In this market, the truth is no longer the benchmark. Influence is.

These mercenaries have reshaped the information battlespace. They are not an outlier—they are a business model. Their proliferation ensures that even states or organizations without internal propaganda infrastructure can project narrative power invisibly and effectively.

## Data Grooming and Poisoning {#data-grooming-and-poisoning}

As artificial intelligence systems become increasingly embedded in how societies generate, store, and retrieve knowledge, adversarial actors have begun to shape AI behavior by corrupting the inputs, both during training and fine-tuning. This tactic, identified by the American Sunlight Project in 2025, is known as Large Language Model (LLM) grooming. \[8\]

LLM grooming refers to the intentional seeding of strategic information manipulation into open internet sources that large language models (LLMs) ingest. Unlike conventional data poisoning, which is designed to break systems, LLM grooming seeks to steer them, biasing outputs by shaping what models learn from. The target isn’t the user. It’s the teacher.

This approach diverges from traditional information manipulation campaigns, which aim to influence human audiences. Instead, it targets the epistemic infrastructure of machines. The goal is to silently embed regime narratives, distorted frames, or falsified contexts into widely scraped sources, such as Wikipedia, public archives, newswire repositories, and scientific databases, knowing that these systems feed foundational models.

ASP’s investigation found that Russian-affiliated content farms, shell media outlets, and translation proxies have quietly saturated public knowledge spaces with Kremlin-aligned narratives on Ukraine, NATO, civil liberties, and historical events [\[9,10\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/SsbwL+Milo0). These texts often mimic credible references—adopting a neutral tone, structured citations, and institutional formatting to increase their odds of inclusion in training datasets.

The impact is already visible. A 2025 study in Nature Medicine demonstrated that even modest amounts of misleading clinical data introduced into LLM training pipelines can lead to unsafe or inaccurate health recommendations, while still appearing authoritative [\[11\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/easjl). These outputs aren’t flagged because the issue lies upstream. The model trusts what it has been taught.

Bowen et al. (2024) explain the cost efficiency: Scaling laws for LLMs suggest that even a few well-placed poisoned examples, especially when repeated with slight variations, can have a disproportionate influence on generalization [\[12\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/CKukK). The goal isn’t volume. It’s strategic repetition.

Additional research, including DFRLab’s analysis of the Pravda network, confirms that hostile actors embed distorted articles into high-trust environments, such as Wikipedia, which are often scraped wholesale for AI training [\[13\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/Iv66G). These sources become part of the scaffolding that models use to assess credibility and context.

The long-term risk is epistemic capture. When AI systems ingest consistent distortions—ideological, geopolitical, or scientific—those patterns aren’t just echoed back; they are amplified. They’re reinforced, normalized, and legitimized [\[14\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/hcJif). LLM grooming isn’t about influencing a single user. It’s about defining the limits of what a system considers proper, or even debatable.

Curriculum design principles apply. By manipulating what models ingest, adversaries shape how future outputs frame topics of strategic interest. This includes what is emphasized, what is omitted, and how contested facts are framed.

The danger is compounded by subtlety. Poisoned content is rarely outright false. It may be selectively accurate, deliberately omissive, or carefully framed to reinforce a preferred worldview. Once incorporated into training data, it becomes difficult to remove. Detection is hard. Retraction is expensive. 

Mitigation requires adversarial data hygiene, which includes auditing source provenance, weighting content from trusted institutions, and actively filtering for coordinated manipulation. Governments and research coalitions must begin treating public knowledge infrastructure as critical to AI security. Civil society must demand transparency in model training disclosures, because future reliability depends on what was seeded years earlier.

LLM grooming isn’t speculative. It’s operational. It’s already embedded in the training pipelines of current systems. It marks the next evolution in information warfare: not just seeding narratives, but quietly redesigning the systems that generate them.

## Influencer Networks {#influencer-networks}

Influencer networks are coordinated groups of online personas—factual, semi-real, or fabricated—used to cross-validate and amplify regime-aligned narratives. Their strength lies in perceived independence: each voice seems unique, but the messaging is harmonized.

When influencers quote each other, cite the same studies, or appear across multiple platforms with shared talking points, they create the illusion of popular agreement. The tactic builds perceived credibility by manufacturing peer validation.

This is not mass deception—it’s reputational inflation through synthetic endorsement.

Real-world examples illustrate the impact of such networks. The Syria Campaign's report, "Deadly Disinformation," highlights how coordinated information manipulation campaigns have targeted Syrian humanitarians and human rights defenders, spreading false narratives about events like chemical attacks and the work of the White Helmets [\[15–17\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/7yfuv+hXImw+Q6GsT). These campaigns often involve influencers who disseminate misleading information, contributing to confusion and undermining international responses.

In Canada, research by the Canadian Digital Media Research Network uncovered how Russian-funded social media content spread through influencer networks, reaching audiences far beyond the original accounts [\[18\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/osEDB). Influencers associated with Tenet Media, such as Lauren Chen and Lauren Southern, were identified as part of this network, illustrating how influencers can be leveraged to disseminate foreign propaganda [\[19–21\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/IVFnV+riDVt+4Veke). 

These cases illustrate the strategic utilization of influencer networks in contemporary information manipulation campaigns, where blending authentic and manipulated voices creates a potent tool for shaping public perception.

## Mil-Bloggers {#mil-bloggers}

Military bloggers—particularly those rooted in ultranationalist, veteran, or ex-military circles—operate as potent amplifiers of state-sanctioned narratives. Frequently styled as independent voices or patriotic dissenters, these actors provide regime narratives with a vital asset: the appearance of authenticity [\[22\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/K2VPh). Their informal tone and battlefield credibility give them cultural capital that official spokespersons often lack.

Mil-bloggers perform a dual role: they simulate autonomous critique while reinforcing the state’s strategic frames [\[23\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/djpMh). Their content often includes performative grievances or tactical-level frustrations—condemning logistical missteps or battlefield losses—but the overarching messages remain aligned with core regime imperatives: defending the war effort, demonizing the adversary, reframing military failures as moral victories, and endorsing hard-line leadership [\[24–26\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/E4ehy+wtIw6+tY4Hi).

This controlled volatility serves a larger psychological operation. By permitting limited internal dissent within acceptable ideological boundaries, the state fosters an illusion of pluralism while containing more dangerous critiques. As a result, mil-bloggers become semi-detached extensions of the state's voice—rhetorically unruly, yet strategically loyal. They fulfill the Kremlin’s long-favored model of “plausible deniability by proxy,” weaponizing civil society actors as agents of influence without official attribution.

## Organized Criminal Activity {#organized-criminal-activity}

Russian organized crime operates not as an external threat but as an integral component of the state's strategic apparatus. These criminal networks are embedded within the informal power structure, serving as instruments of influence and coercion. They undertake tasks the state cannot openly perform, such as funding covert operations, intimidating dissidents, executing cyberattacks, and managing clandestine logistics.

Under the framework of hybrid warfare, organized crime groups function as strategic auxiliaries. Their extralegal status grants them flexibility and deniability, allowing them to operate across borders and through layers of proxies. This makes them effective in activities ranging from money laundering to targeting opposition figures abroad. Individuals often move between roles in state structures, private security firms, and criminal enterprises, blurring the lines between official agents and criminals.

These actors act as coercive entrepreneurs, subcontracting psychological and kinetic influence functions that would be politically hazardous for the state to claim directly. Far from being a liability, organized criminal groups are strategic co-conspirators—embedded, expendable, and deeply functional in projecting state-aligned influence.

In the context of the Russia-Ukraine conflict, Ukrainian organized crime groups have recalibrated their alliances, siding with Ukraine to preserve their autonomy against the threat of absorption by Russian counterparts. This strategic realignment highlights the intricate interplay between national interests and criminal enterprises during wartime.

The integration of organized crime into statecraft extends beyond traditional activities. Criminal networks facilitate the evasion of sanctions, cyber warfare, and intelligence operations. They are instrumental in smuggling sanctioned goods, conducting cyberattacks, and gathering intelligence, effectively acting as extensions of state power in the gray zones of international conflict.

This symbiotic relationship between the state and organized crime challenges conventional distinctions between criminality and governance, highlighting the need for a nuanced understanding and strategic responses to such hybrid threats.

## Social Media Manipulation {#social-media-manipulation}

The manipulation of social media ecosystems constitutes one of the most adaptive, cost-effective, and low-risk tools in modern adversarial operations. At its core lies coordinated inauthentic behavior—the orchestration of botnets, sockpuppet accounts, troll farms, and AI-generated content designed to simulate organic engagement. These networks do not merely amplify messages; they fabricate the illusion of public consensus, engineer virality, and suppress dissenting views through saturation and swarm tactics.

Botnets and troll accounts serve multiple strategic purposes. They can manufacture consent by flooding comment sections with supportive messaging, creating a distorted sense of majority opinion. Alternatively, they are deployed to harass or delegitimize critics, using mass reporting tools to trigger platform suspensions or to intimidate voices into self-censorship. The goal is not persuasion but dominance of the information environment—what the Soviets once termed "reflexive control,": shaping how targets think by controlling the stimuli they are exposed to.

Another key method is the astroturfing of digital discourse. By mimicking grassroots movements, operatives can seed fringe or state-aligned narratives into communities of influence. Hashtags are co-opted, memes are weaponized, and emotionally charged content is algorithmically boosted to provoke engagement. This exploits the core architecture of platforms like Twitter/X, Facebook, and TikTok, where engagement metrics—not factual accuracy—determine reach. Through tactical amplification, otherwise marginal narratives can achieve visibility disproportionate to their genuine support.

Moreover, algorithmic manipulation plays a pivotal role. Influence operators engage in deliberate gaming of recommender systems and trend algorithms. This includes timing posts to peak hours, cross-platform seeding of content to trigger automated detection systems, and using coordinated engagement pods to inflate metrics. These activities create artificial virality, allowing adversarial actors to hijack the platform’s own feedback loops, making extremist or manipulated content appear popular or relevant when it is neither.

This manipulation often operates across linguistic, cultural, and ideological boundaries. Sophisticated campaigns may tailor content in different languages to target specific demographic or political segments, ranging from nationalist groups to environmental activists, adapting tone and narrative to resonate with local grievances. This granular targeting enhances narrative penetration, allowing the same core message (anti-Western sentiment, anti-NATO discourse) to manifest in ideologically diverse forms.

Finally, the emergence of deepfake technologies and generative AI introduces a new dimension. Now, information manipulation campaigns can deploy realistic synthetic personas, video forgeries, or auto-generated op-eds, reducing reliance on human operators and increasing scalability. Such tools enable persistent influence operations that are harder to trace, verify, or counter, especially in under-moderated information environments.

What results is not merely the dissemination of a message, but the reframing of reality itself. Social media manipulation erodes epistemic trust, blurs the boundary between authenticity and fiction, and renders public discourse more susceptible to tribalism and cynicism. The weaponization of virality thus becomes a form of soft coercion: a cognitive battlefield in which visibility, not veracity, determines victory.

## State-Aligned Social Groups {#state-aligned-social-groups}

While often dismissed as marginal or symbolic, state-aligned social organizations operating abroad have become central tools in the contemporary arsenal of authoritarian influence. Unlike traditional public diplomacy or cultural outreach, these groups are not engaged in bridge-building—they are deployed to penetrate, destabilize, and polarize foreign societies, often by infiltrating and exploiting local grievance movements. Their key asset is narrative mimicry: they appear to advocate for human rights, anti-colonialism, or social justice, but do so in ways that align with and launder the geopolitical interests of the sponsoring regime.

The case of Russian activist Aleksandr Ionov and his Antiglobalization Movement of Russia (AGMR) exemplifies this model. Founded with Kremlin blessing in 2012, AGMR brands itself as a solidarity platform for oppressed peoples, supporting independence movements, anti-NATO rhetoric, and resistance to Western imperialism. It operates as a clearinghouse for foreign influence operations, offering a platform for fringe or radical groups in the West to receive material support, ideological guidance, and international legitimacy from Moscow.

Ionov, who maintained ties to Russian security agencies and state-backed foundations, actively courted far-left, separatist, and pan-Africanist groups in the United States, Latin America, and Africa. U.S. Department of Justice indictments in 2022 and 2023 revealed that he served as an agent of foreign influence, directly coordinating with the FSB and using his organization to fund and direct American political activists. Notably within Black nationalist, socialist, and anti-imperialist circles. Among these were:

* **The Uhuru Movement / African People's Socialist Party (APSP)**: Targeted and allegedly financed by Ionov to issue pro-Russian statements about the Ukraine war, organize protests, and run candidates for local office.

* **The Black Hammer Organization**: A radical group that received visibility through ties to AGMR and adopted openly anti-U.S., pro-Russian messaging, including praise for Vladimir Putin and support for Russia’s “denazification” narrative in Ukraine.

These groups did not merely act as conduits for messaging. They were operationalized—used to stage protests, file petitions, publish propaganda, and contest elections, all in service of a foreign power’s strategic narrative. The genius of this method lies in its camouflage: domestic dissent is leveraged as international pressure. By embedding themselves within authentic struggles, such as racial injustice, anti-capitalism, and decolonization, state-aligned actors can deflect scrutiny and portray their interventions as grassroots solidarity, rather than foreign subversion.

In essence, these are proxy movements with geopolitical payloads. They serve as ideological mercenaries, recruited not for loyalty to a foreign state but for alignment with specific narratives, such as anti-Westernism, anti-NATO sentiment, and multipolar resistance. AGMR and similar bodies provide these groups with a ready-made ideological framework, international recognition, media amplification (often via RT or Sputnik), and sometimes covert funding.

This model of external mobilization represents an evolution of the Soviet-era “active measures” doctrine, but with a 21st-century twist. Instead of tightly controlled Communist front groups, the new generation of state-aligned civic operators functions more like ideological franchise networks. Their appeal is broad, their branding local, but their infrastructure is centrally supported. By piggybacking onto democratic openness and civil liberties, authoritarian states can weaponize pluralism against itself, turning the languages of emancipation and justice into tools of subversion.

This strategy is not unique to Russia. Comparable tactics have been observed in Chinese United Front-linked organizations, Iranian revolutionary export committees, and Turkish diaspora watchdogs. What distinguishes the Russian model, however, is its emphasis on ideological chaos rather than coherent doctrine. Its goal is not to win converts to Russian nationalism, but to fracture the cognitive and political cohesion of adversarial societies—to amplify distrust, cynicism, and internal contradiction.

In short, these groups are not simply “analog bots.” They are geopolitical insurgents cloaked in activist garb—networked, plausible, and increasingly compelling. Their existence demands new frameworks for understanding influence, sovereignty, and civic trust in an age where loyalty is not transactional, but narrative-aligned and post-ideological.

## State-Content Aggregators {#state-content-aggregators}

State-content aggregators represent a subtler vector of narrative influence—platforms that compile, reframe, and redistribute regime-aligned or proxy-produced content within broader, ideologically coded media ecosystems. Unlike traditional outlets that originate news, these sites specialize in selecting and amplifying materials that align with their target audience’s worldview—libertarian, populist, conspiratorial, or anti-establishment—while routinely injecting foreign information manipulation into the bloodstream of “alternative” media.

State-content aggregators do not obscure the origin of regime-produced material—they normalize it. Platforms such as Zero Hedge, Blacklisted News, and LewRockwell.com frequently publish content from overt state media sources, including RT, Sputnik, and Press TV. These sources are not hidden—they’re integrated, embedded, and presented alongside other ideological material to convey that their viewpoints are part of a legitimate, pluralistic discourse.

The function isn’t to obfuscate the source; it’s contextual laundering. The aggregator doesn’t hide where content comes from; instead, it subtly alters what that content means by placing it in a curated ecosystem of distrust toward mainstream institutions. A link to RT isn’t framed as “Kremlin propaganda”—it’s offered as “a perspective the mainstream won’t show you.” In this way, state-aligned narratives gain proximity to audiences predisposed to anti-establishment, anti-elite, or anti-Western sentiments—not through deception, but through ideological resonance.

The real sleight of hand lies in framing and juxtaposition. Aggregator platforms assemble stories about government overreach, economic collapse, vaccine controversies, and surveillance states—then position RT headlines or Russian Ministry of Defense press releases as corroborating evidence. When placed beside libertarian op-eds or “deep state” exposés, these state narratives appear less like foreign influence and more like confirmation. This creates an aura of legitimacy, not because the source is disguised, but because the reader has already been primed to view the establishment as inherently untrustworthy.

In this context, RT doesn’t need to hide. Its presence on Zero Hedge or similar platforms signals not foreign allegiance but independent thinking, contrarian insight, or radical truth-telling. Readers may interpret it as, “They must be saying something the elites don’t want you to hear.”

By embedding state narratives within a broader stream of ideological content, such as market analyses, alternative health posts, and civil liberties commentary, they reposition themselves as part of a wider populist or oppositional worldview. Readers who might scoff at state media in isolation are more willing to entertain its claims when they’re part of a constellation of messages that already match their priors.

So, while state media links are not hidden, they are reframed, and their presence is normalized. Aggregators turn what would otherwise be blatant propaganda into “just another article” in a media diet designed for skepticism, outrage, and ideological cohesion. The effect is not to deceive the audience about where the content originates—it’s to alter how the audience perceives the source when it arrives.

In short, state-content aggregators don’t erase provenance—they erase stigma. In doing so, they function as the middle layer in a larger information control stack. Aggregators provide volume while also creating the impression that these outlets are just like all the other contrarian voices..

## State-Media Partnerships {#state-media-partnerships}

State-media partnerships are strategic collaborations between national governments and media outlets, often extending beyond borders, to co-publish or syndicate content. These alliances enable states to disseminate narratives through platforms perceived as independent or neutral, thereby enhancing the credibility and reach of their messages. By embedding state-sponsored content within trusted media environments, these partnerships can increase the perceived credibility of the foreign outlets.

A notable example is China's engagement with international media. The China Global Television Network (CGTN), the international arm of China's state broadcaster China Central Television (CCTV), has collaborated with foreign media outlets to co-produce content that aligns with Chinese state narratives. For instance, CGTN co-produced a documentary with the Discovery Channel titled "World’s Ultimate Frontier," which portrayed the Uyghur region in Xinjiang as a thriving area, contrasting sharply with reports of human rights abuses in the region. Such co-productions allow China to influence global perceptions by leveraging the credibility of established media brands [\[27,28\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/jXzMy+F3roQ).

Similarly, Indonesia's state news agency, Antara, has partnered with Chinese state media outlets like Xinhua News Agency and China Global Television Network [\[29\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/u8EWs). These agreements enable Antara to distribute Chinese state-produced content, amplifying China's narratives within Indonesia and potentially influencing public opinion through a local media channel.

## Train-the-Trainers {#train-the-trainers}

Train-the-trainers programs build local capacity to repeat and adapt regime-aligned messaging. Sympathetic individuals receive ideological, cultural, or technical training and are embedded multipliers within their networks.

Train-the-trainers programs are foundational tactics in long-term influence operations. Rather than relying on mass persuasion or overt propaganda, these programs embed influence agents within the social fabric of a target state. The approach focuses on deep, localized seeding of regime-aligned narratives by cultivating sympathetic individuals who are trained to reinforce strategic messaging within their communities, often including educators, clergy, activists, or youth leaders.

In Moldova, the 2024–2025 election and constitutional referendum presented a real-world demonstration of this method. According to Moldova’s Intelligence and Security Service (SIS), Russian intelligence services oversaw a large-scale train-the-trainers operation designed to undermine Moldova’s European integration and destabilize the state’s democratic institutions [\[30\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/m66LA).

At the center of the operation was Ilan Șor, a fugitive Moldovan oligarch convicted of banking fraud and operating from exile in Russia [\[31\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/fOnEY). Working closely with Russia’s FSB (specifically the 5th Service) [\[32\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/4GoUf), Șor coordinated a dual-track strategy: a political movement (Pobeda–Victorie bloc) and a civic front (the NGO Evrazia) [\[33,34\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/N6zTB+cON0j). Both served as cover for training and deploying regime-aligned influencers throughout Moldovan society.

The scale of the program was unprecedented for Moldova. Over 100 coordinators trained by Șor’s political machine were deployed to manage 119 regional cells [\[31,35\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/GXs6f+fOnEY). These cells, in turn, organized over 33,000 activists, each tasked with persuading 5–10 voters using material and ideological incentives. Many of these activists had undergone training in Moscow, which was presented as a cultural exchange or civic education trip through Evrazia [\[35,36\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/GXs6f+DHXXj).

According to SIS intelligence, the curriculum for these sessions went far beyond messaging. It included tactical instruction on organizing protests, confronting police, coordinating youth outreach, and using social media for narrative diffusion. Some participants were taught how to provoke and film police interactions for propaganda purposes, or how to neutralize surveillance and co-opt civic leaders.

Trainers from Russian political circles, including the “Young Guard of United Russia,” and Moldovan diaspora organizations, were co-opted by the Evrazia network [\[30,35\]](https://paperpile.com/c/ZvLlgp/GXs6f+m66LA). The SIS also reported that select trainees were funneled to additional programs in Bosnia and Herzegovina—allegedly guerrilla camps—where they received instruction in drone deployment, psychological operations during protests, and recruitment psychology.

What distinguishes the Moldovan case is not only the scope but also the durability of the tactic. Once trained, these actors return home as “organic” influencers. They blend into the institutional landscape—civil society, education, religion, local media—and their messaging appears locally generated even when aligned with Russian strategic objectives.

This is the essence of train-the-trainers: strategic recursion. It enables a hostile actor to convert a narrow set of operatives into a self-replicating influence structure. Over time, these networks become difficult to dismantle because they are not centralized. They are distributed, informal, and fused with genuine civic identities.

Moldova's experience in 2024 and 2025 shows how this model operates at scale, across platforms, and under institutional cover. It involves the deliberate cultivation of influence agents armed not with slogans but legitimacy.
