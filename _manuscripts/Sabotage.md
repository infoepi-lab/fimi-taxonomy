# Sabotage

Sabotage functions are explicitly destructive. They seek to undermine
adversaries not by convincing or co-opting, but by destabilizing,
dividing, or degrading. These include exporting corruption, strategic
lawfare, and covert criminal acts. Demoralization campaigns and
disinformation fracturing generate confusion, distrust, and paralysis.
Amplifying extremism, falsifying historical documents, or stoking
intergroup conflict fragments social trust and institutional integrity.
Sabotage is not always visible but is often the most corrosive, aiming
to erode the pillars of democratic and social resilience from within.

# Overview {#overview}

Sabotage refers to the deliberate degradation of a target society’s
internal cohesion, decision-making capacity, or epistemic stability
through indirect, non-kinetic means. It is not necessarily destructive
in the traditional sense, but corrosive—eroding the connective tissue of
a polity from within. While it may appear incidental or decentralized,
sabotage functions as a strategic weapon in the broader architecture of
hybrid warfare, using information, psychology, and institutional
vulnerabilities to destabilize from the inside out. This section
outlines six core mechanisms through which sabotage operates as an
instrument of state influence.

# Varieties {#varieties}

## Strategic Corruption {#strategic-corruption}

Strategic corruption refers to the strategic dissemination of corrupt
norms, practices, and institutional arrangements beyond a state’s
borders to secure influence, cultivate dependency, and establish
mechanisms of leverage. Unlike isolated acts of bribery, this is not
incidental—it is systemic, intentional, and embedded within a broader
foreign policy logic. Authoritarian regimes, particularly those whose
domestic governance rests on opaque networks of patronage and control,
project these models outward as a means of weakening foreign
institutions and establishing durable channels of coercion.

Rather than promoting transparent trade or aid, these regimes use opaque
contracts, off-the-books financial transfers, and captured elites to
transform what appear to be economic relationships into mechanisms of
political control. Through public-private intermediaries, state-backed
firms, and diplomatic missions, they replicate at the international
level the same fusion of wealth, power, and unaccountability that
underpins their domestic rule.

Often, this involves cultivating local political actors, business
leaders, or bureaucrats who are willing to exchange regulatory favors,
access, or silence for financial gain. Once compromised, these actors
become points of influence within the host country—individuals whose
decision-making is shaped not by public interest but by external
alignment. This creates a structural vulnerability: when these networks
are deep enough, the foreign actor can delay legislation, suppress
investigations, shape elections, or sabotage collective responses to
malign activity—all from within the legal and bureaucratic framework of
the target state.

Strategic corruption functions as both offense and defense. Offensively,
it extends the regime’s reach into the decision-making spaces of other
states. Defensively, it blunts democratic scrutiny by creating a class
of compromised insiders who resist accountability or reform. These
networks are difficult to expose because they are often laundered
through legitimate-looking business ventures or channeled via
third-party jurisdictions, NGOs, or nominally private companies.

Unlike traditional espionage, which depends on secrecy, strategic
corruption thrives in plain sight, embedded in procurement documents,
lobbying contracts, and development finance agreements. Once normalized,
it becomes self-sustaining—transforming corruption from a liability into
a geostrategic asset.

### **Examples** {#examples}

In the Western Balkans, Russian entities have used energy contracts,
opaque real estate purchases, and favorable loans to entangle elites in
Montenegro, Serbia, and Bosnia-Herzegovina, ensuring political alignment
on issues such as NATO integration or EU sanctions.

In Sub-Saharan Africa, Chinese- and Russian-linked companies have
offered extractive deals with hidden ownership structures and kickbacks
to officials, producing elite capture while undermining anti-corruption
efforts.

In EU member states such as Hungary, strategic infrastructure projects
involving Russian state-owned enterprises have come with allegations of
favoritism, shell companies, and unexplained financial flows.

The case of Austria’s Freedom Party (FPÖ) illustrates another vector:
covert financial support and personal enrichment deals (as revealed in
the 2019 “Ibiza Affair”) that blur the line between campaign finance and
foreign influence.

## Stoking Intergroup Conflict {#stoking-intergroup-conflict}

Stoking intergroup conflict is a sabotage tactic aimed at fracturing
societal cohesion by amplifying or fabricating tensions between distinct
identity groups—ethnic, religious, linguistic, ideological, or social.
Rather than promoting a coherent ideological alternative, this mechanism
thrives on division, mistrust, and escalated grievance, creating an
environment in which adversaries become preoccupied with internal
discord. This inhibits unified responses to external threats and erodes
confidence in state institutions.

The strategic logic is both psychological and structural. By
destabilizing the target society from within, the aggressor ensures that
state attention and resources are diverted inward, public trust
deteriorates, and openings are created for further manipulation,
coercion, or opportunistic intervention. Intergroup antagonism becomes a
force multiplier—weakening the target polity without confrontation.

### **Ethnic and Nationalist Agitation** {#ethnic-and-nationalist-agitation}

This form involves manipulating historical grievances, border disputes,
or demographic anxieties to provoke ethnic polarization or nationalist
backlash. It often invokes narratives of betrayal, cultural erasure, or
existential threat, framing one group as inherently incompatible with
the nation’s identity or sovereignty.

Russian state-linked actors have amplified separatist narratives in
regions like Donbas, Transnistria, and Abkhazia, portraying ethnic
Russians as victims of discrimination while supporting “self-defense”
movements. In Catalonia, Russian-linked media and trolls inflated
separatist claims to exacerbate internal Spanish divisions, even when
the local movement was nonviolent. Similar tactics have been noted in
Scotland and Bavaria, where exaggerated grievances are used to test the
boundaries of Western federal cohesion.

This variety exploits the symbolic infrastructure of national identity.
By inserting disinformation or promoting symbolic flashpoints (language
laws, memorial debates), adversaries can activate latent divisions.
These operations rarely need to invent conflict; instead, they intensify
existing tensions and add informational accelerants. Crucially, the
intent is not to resolve or even formalize secession—it is to keep the
wound open.

### **Social and Moral Polarization** {#social-and-moral-polarization}

This variety centers on culture war themes—gender, sexuality, religion,
or race—and uses these as wedges to deepen social mistrust. Rather than
inventing new ideologies, it promotes fringe interpretations, elevates
extreme voices, or manufactures controversies that position identity
groups against each other.

Kremlin-aligned outlets and proxies have supported anti-LGBTQ
legislation in Eastern Europe, positioning it as a defense of
“traditional values” against decadent Western liberalism. In the United
States, Russian trolls from the Internet Research Agency simultaneously
amplified Black Lives Matter content and pro-police counter-narratives,
heightening tension rather than advancing either cause. Other campaigns
have included promoting conspiracy theories about immigration
“replacement” or stoking Islamophobia in Western Europe.

These operations aim to collapse the middle ground, forcing target
populations into reactive identity postures. Polarization of this type
is difficult to counter because it presents itself as grassroots
activism or moral conviction. Yet its true function is
sabotage—diverting attention from governance, neutralizing policy
consensus, and undermining pluralist norms. By inflaming these
divisions, the aggressor degrades not only social trust but also the
cognitive legitimacy of compromise.

# Discussion {#discussion}

Stoking intergroup conflict is low-cost, deniable, and highly adaptive.
It does not require a coherent ideology, a formal proxy force, or direct
intervention. It only requires access to the information ecosystem and a
working knowledge of societal fault lines. Once these are identified,
amplification techniques—social media manipulation, sympathetic local
actors, covert funding—can do the rest.

What makes this function particularly insidious is its ability to
exploit democratic openness. Free speech protections, decentralized
media, and the proliferation of online platforms all allow adversarial
actors to amplify divisions under the guise of dialogue. Moreover,
because the content often aligns with domestic grievances or real
events, it becomes harder to distinguish authentic dissent from
manipulated provocation.

In short, this is sabotage by proxy—not to replace the political order,
but to rupture its capacity to cohere. It is not revolution, but
entropy. And it leaves behind a poisoned environment in which any
response to foreign manipulation can be reframed as domestic repression,
further feeding the cycle.

## Demoralization Campaigns {#demoralization-campaigns}

Demoralization campaigns operate by strategically sapping the willpower,
confidence, and collective resolve of adversary populations. Their goal
is not to persuade, inspire, or recruit, but to paralyze. By flooding
the information environment with despairing, defeatist, or nihilistic
content, these campaigns reduce the psychological capacity of societies
to resist external pressure, reform internal weaknesses, or maintain
coherent national purpose.

Where traditional propaganda seeks loyalty, demoralization cultivates
apathy, disengagement, and emotional exhaustion. It disables—not through
censorship, but through saturation.

### **Examples** {#examples-1}

In Ukraine, Russian-aligned Telegram channels have circulated fake
casualty videos, doctored surrender footage, and exaggerated territorial
losses to weaken both civilian morale and military cohesion. These were
often timed to coincide with kinetic operations or diplomatic
escalations, ensuring that public perception of failure preceded or
exceeded reality.

In the broader Euro-Atlantic context, Russian and aligned disinformation
outlets have repeatedly advanced the narrative that “the West is
collapsing.” Through curated stories of racial conflict, energy crises,
inflation, political paralysis, and cultural decay, these campaigns
frame democratic societies as irreversibly broken. The goal is to
portray liberal governance not as flawed but as fundamentally unviable.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, foreign actors exploited death tolls,
lockdown disputes, and vaccine misinformation to depict democratic
governments as incapable of protecting their citizens—thus reinforcing
narratives of systemic failure.

### **Discussion** {#discussion-1}

Demoralization is a sabotage technique that targets the affective
infrastructure of public life. Its operational vector is despair. It
exploits pluralistic information environments, where critique is not
just allowed but celebrated, by selectively amplifying the most
pessimistic, disunifying, or defeatist narratives available. Over time,
this saturates the cognitive field with futility.

The intended result is emotional disengagement—a public too overwhelmed,
cynical, or numb to act. In democratic systems, this can produce
electoral apathy, disillusionment with reform, or openness to
authoritarian alternatives. In wartime, it can preemptively degrade
morale and lower resistance without requiring confrontation.

Unlike lies or false flags, demoralization campaigns often rely on real
data, real failures, real pain—strategically edited, sequenced, and
contextualized to produce maximum hopelessness. That is what makes them
both harder to refute and more corrosive over time.

## Information Fracturing {#information-fracturing}

Information fracturing is the deliberate dissemination of multiple,
conflicting narratives into the information environment to disorient
audiences and degrade their ability to distinguish truth from falsehood.
Unlike classical disinformation, which advances a singular false
narrative, this technique operates by flooding the zone—introducing a
chaotic mix of half-truths, contradictions, and speculative claims
[1](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/Ps8Ia). The objective is not
persuasion but cognitive overload
[2,3](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/7gJ5Y+aeoO7). When individuals are
exposed to a glut of incompatible explanations, they become more likely
to rely on trusted sources [4](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/lLAxl).
This may be true whether the source is credible or even appropriate for
the topic.

This method thrives in moments of crisis or confusion, when demand for
reliable information is high but verification is difficult
[5](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/vync3). Rather than resolve
uncertainty, information fracturing sustains and weaponizes it,
undermining the very conditions required for informed public judgment.

### **Examples** {#examples-2}

A notable instance of information fracturing occurred after the downing
of Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 in 2014. Russian state and proxy media
circulated a barrage of divergent explanations—Ukrainian jet fighters,
mistaken identity, a CIA conspiracy—none of which were substantiated
[6–8](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/VHdAK+fXevq+wtB6e). The goal was
not to establish a convincing counter-narrative but to create so many
competing versions that the truth was lost in the noise
[9](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/SJkGS).

Similar techniques have been employed in the aftermath of chemical
weapons attacks in Syria, where Russian and Syrian regime-aligned
outlets offered multiple, mutually contradictory theories—false-flag
operations, staged videos, accidental gas leaks—casting doubt on
responsibility and paralyzing international response
[10–13](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/St2W1+xVgIF+OfVCl+VfIe3).

In the COVID-19 pandemic, foreign actors injected conflicting narratives
about the virus’s origin (lab leak vs. bioweapon vs. zoonotic), the
reliability of vaccines, and the intentions of public health authorities
[14–18](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/GQnRg+QIHAK+0PmaX+ngJ2D+krA7k).
And when U.S. leadership began implying that SARS-CoV-2 was a biological
weapon, it kicked off a narratives arms race
[19](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/n2Fz7). The collateral damage what
the public’s ability to differentiate signal from noise.

### **Discussion** {#discussion-2}

Information fracturing corrodes not only truth but the very architecture
of knowability. It is especially destructive in democratic societies,
where the public sphere depends on a shared baseline of facts to make
collective decisions. By overwhelming discourse with contradictions, the
attacker erodes institutional legitimacy, reduces the salience of expert
consensus, and encourages disengagement or hyper-partisanship.

This variety of sabotage offers autocratic regimes a strategic
advantage. Where democracies require transparency and truth to function,
authoritarian actors benefit from ambiguity and doubt. When no narrative
dominates, they are free to assert control over perception, dismiss
accusations as speculative, and deflect scrutiny indefinitely.

At scale, information fracturing creates an epistemic smog in which
denial becomes plausible, and the very notion of factual accountability
is weakened. It is not a battle of ideas—it is the collapse of the
conditions for debate itself.

## Document and Archive Manipulation {#document-and-archive-manipulation}

Document and archive manipulation is a sabotage technique that targets
the evidentiary foundations of truth. It involves the fabrication,
alteration, suppression, or selective presentation of official
documents, historical records, or archival material to construct false
narratives, obscure responsibility, or launder disinformation through
seemingly authoritative channels.

This technique exploits the perceived permanence and legitimacy of
documents—state memos, intelligence files, historical archives, or
journalistic records—to embed disinformation into the factual record.
Once introduced, these distortions can be recycled over decades, shaping
how events are remembered, adjudicated, or understood. The aim is not to
generate temporary confusion but to reshape institutional memory and
legitimize fiction as fact.

### **Examples** {#examples-3}

During the Cold War, the KGB’s “Operation INFEKTION” involved forging a
document purporting to be a classified U.S. military report claiming the
Pentagon created the AIDS virus as a bioweapon
[20–23](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/9xJSS+wuSoE+soEXI+8dgFU). This
fabricated memo was planted in a fringe Indian newspaper and eventually
picked up by international outlets. The disinformation was later
rebroadcast by Soviet domestic media, giving it the appearance of
external confirmation—a technique known as narrative laundering.

Soviet intelligence also fabricated “CIA memos” implicating U.S.
diplomats in assassination plots or regime-change conspiracies, which
were inserted into European media to discredit American foreign policy
[24–27](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/QFkwM+f8uHZ+WFp8a+brjyR). These
forgeries were rarely meant to hold up under scrutiny; their power lay
in initial impact and cumulative erosion of credibility.

More recently, Russian military intelligence (GRU) has been implicated
in the manipulation of digital forensic trails, such as planting
misleading metadata in leaked documents or editing captured
communications to distort their meaning before public release
[25,28](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/f8uHZ+reKvC).

### **Discussion** {#discussion-3}

This variety of sabotage weaponizes the very tools designed to preserve
accountability. By corrupting the documentary record, attackers do not
merely distort public discourse—they reshape the substrate on which
future truth claims will be judged. This can obstruct international
investigations, delay legal proceedings, and create plausible
deniability for war crimes, covert actions, or domestic repression.

In the digital era, this technique has only grown more potent. Archival
manipulation now extends to hacked document dumps, selective leaks, and
deepfake textual artifacts. The goal is not only to lie, but to embed
the lie in a medium that appears neutral, static, and verifiable.

Document and archive manipulation is especially corrosive when deployed
against liberal institutions that rely on transparency, documentation,
and rule-of-law procedures. When false documents infiltrate evidence
chains or historical records, the integrity of the truth-seeking process
itself comes under attack.

Ultimately, this is sabotage not of infrastructure or personnel, but of
epistemic trust—the slow poisoning of the record upon which justice,
history, and governance depend. In a world where anyone can say, “That's
fake,” accountability cannot exist.

## Boosting Extremism {#boosting-extremism}

Boosting extremism involves the covert or deniable support of radical
political movements, fringe ideologies, or polarizing social actors in a
target society. This variety of sabotage leverages extremist groups not
for their ideological compatibility, but for their destabilizing
potential. By amplifying divisive voices, regimes can fracture political
consensus, discredit democratic institutions, and increase
volatility—all while concealing the foreign origin of the support.

Support can take multiple forms: covert funding, material assistance,
media amplification, signal boosting via bot networks, or the
coordination of disinformation campaigns that elevate fringe actors
above their organic influence level. These groups are not necessarily
created by foreign regimes—but once identified, they are
instrumentalized as political accelerants.

### **Examples** {#examples-4}

Russia has provided material, financial, or covert media support to
far-right parties in Europe, including Alternative für Deutschland (AfD)
in Germany and National Rally (formerly Front National) in France
[29–33](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/jlvcR+2hLP6+HntTU+r9Wcq+NVZGY).
These parties promote Euroscepticism, anti-immigration policies, and
strategic realignment away from NATO—all aligning with Kremlin
interests, regardless of their domestic origins
[34,35](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/BBNdI+NSXWL).

In the United States, Kremlin-linked actors have supported both
far-right and far-left extremist narratives. One notable case involved
the Black Hammer Organization, a fringe Black nationalist group that
received support tied to Russian influence networks in exchange for
staging public demonstrations and producing propaganda targeting U.S.
institutions [36–38](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/lIN6z+7n2ky+XcZPZ).
Meanwhile, online campaigns amplified both white nationalist and radical
anti-police messaging—not to support either cause, but to exacerbate
internal polarization
[39–41](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/wRmXO+siFES+pfxEG).

In the Balkans, Russian state-connected actors have bolstered
nationalist paramilitary groups and Orthodox clerical ultraconservatives
to fuel ethnic tensions and obstruct Euro-Atlantic integration
[42–45](https://paperpile.com/c/A94rOJ/gs9t2+8a0OU+B3uh0+K6iU8).

### **Discussion** {#discussion-4}

Boosting extremism is a low-cost, high-impact tactic. It relies on
identifying social fissures, then inflating marginal voices into
disproportionate disruptors. These groups often act as political
contaminants, pushing mainstream parties into more radical positions or
provoking reactionary responses that deepen polarization.

This technique is particularly insidious because the foreign hand is
rarely visible. Extremist actors often appear to be organic expressions
of local dissent. Yet their amplification is regularly the result of
covert external curation—a manipulation of the democratic ecosystem that
disguises foreign sabotage as domestic upheaval.

Over time, the effect is corrosive: political discourse shifts to
extremes, trust in centrist institutions erodes, and societies begin to
lose the capacity for consensus or pluralist governance. Importantly,
the foreign actor does not need these extremists to win elections or
seize power—their value lies in the chaos they introduce.

Boosting extremism weaponizes the free speech protections and
decentralized media environment of liberal democracies, turning
tolerance into a vector for antagonism. As such, it is not simply
interference—it is sabotage through ideological inflation, where the
loudest voices are made louder not to persuade, but to destabilize.

# 

# Discussion {#discussion-5}

Sabotage, in the context of influence operations, is not episodic. It is
systemic, recursive, and designed to become self-reinforcing. By
embedding friction, confusion, and mistrust into the target society, it
ensures that governance becomes harder, trust more fragile, and reform
less likely. It is the corrosion of adversary capacity through ambient
destabilization, accomplished not with bombs or bullets, but with
narratives, networks, and institutional infection. This is sabotage as
doctrine—not disruption for its own sake, but as preparation of the
terrain for deeper influence or eventual intervention.
