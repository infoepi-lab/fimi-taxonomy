# Beyond Attribution

The phrase “It was Russia” has become both accusation and solution—a
reflexive endpoint to headlines, investigations, and public outrage. It
carries moral weight, yet rarely triggers meaningful countermeasures.

Yes, sometimes it was Russia. *And*?

Russia’s role in malign influence is neither new nor surprising. It is
entrenched, persistent, innovative, and honed over a century. Unmasking
it does little to make it vanish. Among modern states, Russia—and its
Soviet predecessor—stands out as the most adaptive, ideologically
committed, and enduring actor in the global influence arena. It has
built, sustained, and exported a doctrine of information manipulation
designed not merely to shape what societies believe, but how they
believe it.

Its methods are layered and strategic. What often appears chaotic is, in
fact, deliberately flexible and only partially visible. The U.S. tends
to interpret a clear linear progression as planning; when operations
seem disordered, we assume they are disorganized. But creating effects
that are difficult to perceive—or that look improvised—is itself a
strategic advantage.

Russia has mastered ambiguity and opportunism by design. What looks like
improvisation is often a doctrine finely tuned for the open-information
environment.

Our reluctance to confront reality—often in the name of avoiding
sensationalism—comes at a cost. This is a low-visibility, low-casualty,
cost-effective weapon used routinely against us, domestically and
abroad. Despite decades of exposure, documentation, and intermittent
concern, we remain no better—perhaps worse—prepared than before.

An honest assessment of U.S. mitigation reveals a response that is slow,
fragmented, and alarmingly unserious. It stands in stark contrast to
Russia’s hyper-vigilance toward perceived NATO threats. Where Moscow
overreacts theatrically to perceived slights, Washington underreacts to
threats it can plainly observe.

This book is about mapping a doctrine, not sensationalizing it. Not
about seeing Russia behind every protest, outlet, or dissenting voice.
It is about recognizing what happens when Russia walks through the front
door unnoticed—or stands in plain sight speaking someone else’s
language, shaping perceptions in ways we rarely see until it is too
late.

We use examples from the Soviet and Russian systems not because they are
the only actors engaged in malign influence, but because their history
provides an unparalleled body of evidence. While others experimented,
Russia operationalized. Across generations, regimes, and political
systems, it has embedded influence wherever meaning, power, and
perception intersect. What looks ad hoc to an outside observer is often
centuries of learning distilled into techniques of subtle manipulation,
exploiting human trust, societal norms, and institutional gaps.

The time for shock is over. Attribution is necessary—but it is no longer
sufficient. Simply naming the actor does not tell us what matters. The
real questions lie deeper:

-   What is the function of these operations?

-   What are they trying to change in us—our decisions, our trust, our
    cohesion?\

-   How can we detect not just the signal, but the strategy?\

-   How can we educate the public, strengthen institutions, and
    safeguard the foundations of consent and coherence?

This book offers a schema—a map of functions, not a checklist of
threats. Influence is organized not by platform or tactic, but by what
it achieves: to obscure origin, manufacture legitimacy, project
presence, sabotage trust, or amplify effect. Each mechanism is defined
not by appearance, but by outcome. Understanding what influence does is
more important than knowing who wields it.

We begin with Russia because ignoring its history would be analytic
malpractice. Yet our focus moves beyond attribution toward intent,
implementation, and impact. This is not a book about Russian guilt. It
is a book about democratic vulnerability, about how societies built on
transparency, consent, and cohesion can be subtly undermined without a
single shot fired.

Consider the implications. For decades, Russia has refined a system of
influence that targets not just what we believe, but how we come to
believe it. It exploits ambiguity, thrives in opacity, and weaponizes
trust. The methods are diverse—some overt, some hidden; some
hyper-visible, others cloaked in layers of intermediaries and
disinformation. The strategy is always the same: weaken cohesion, shape
narratives, and project power indirectly.

And yet, despite decades of documentation, exposure, and intermittent
alarm, the response from the United States has been sluggish,
fragmented, and insufficient. We react too slowly to subtle influence
and overcompensate for threats that are obvious but manageable. Where
Moscow over-prepares, Washington under-prepares. Where Moscow dramatizes
over minor provocations, Washington shrugs at systemic interference.
This asymmetry is not accidental—it is strategic, and it is costly.

We cannot continue to operate under the illusion that unmasking the
actor is enough. Exposure alone has never stopped these operations, nor
will it. But understanding the mechanics—the how and the why—offers a
path to defense. By dissecting the function of influence, we can
anticipate moves, fortify institutions, and educate the public in ways
that reduce vulnerability.

This is the lens of this book. Not a catalog of scandals or operations,
but a blueprint of functions: how influence operates, how it
manipulates, and how it propagates. We explore patterns that persist
across decades, regimes, and political systems, because understanding
these patterns equips us to recognize influence regardless of the actor.
Whether Russia, another state, or a non-state network, the lessons hold.

The stakes are real. When influence undermines trust in democratic
institutions, erodes confidence in the press, or distorts public
perception, it is not merely a nuisance—it is an existential threat.
Democracies are built on consent, transparency, and cohesion. Each act
of manipulation erodes one or more of these pillars. The cumulative
effect is vulnerability, the kind that is invisible until it is
devastatingly obvious.

If all we know is who did it, we will lose. History shows that exposing
actors has rarely stopped operations. But understanding how influence
works—and why it is wielded—allows us to develop countermeasures. It
allows us to educate, protect, and fortify. It allows us to act before
the damage becomes irreversible.

This book offers a path forward. It begins with Russia, because ignoring
its history is analytically irresponsible. It moves beyond attribution
into strategy, function, and impact. It provides a framework for
understanding influence as a systemic challenge, not a series of
isolated incidents. And it emphasizes the ultimate lesson: resilience,
awareness, and proactive defense are the only effective responses in a
world where influence is as potent a weapon as any conventional tool.

By understanding the architecture of influence, by mapping its
mechanisms, and by recognizing its objectives, we reclaim agency. We
protect not only our institutions, but the very fabric of democratic
society. Because if all we do is identify the actor, we are already
behind. But if we understand how influence shapes reality, we stand a
chance to fight back—effectively, strategically, and enduringly—no
matter who controls the ecosystem tomorrow.

# Attribution

Attribution refers to identifying the sponsor or controlling actor
behind an information asset or operation. Some forms of attribution rely
on verifiable evidence, while others require inference, synthesis, or
indirect signals. Attribution can therefore be divided into two broad
categories: Concrete and Circumstantial. The language in a report should
match the type of evidence.

## Concrete

These forms of attribution rely on observable, legally or technically
verifiable indicators. They require little or no interpretive analysis
and are often accessible to third parties willing to examine the record.

### **Public Data**

Information is openly available through routine observation, open-source
aggregation, or deliberate publication. The sponsor may even emphasize
its role to enhance the influence effect. No clearance, licensing, or
insider access is required.

### **Classified**

Controlled by formal government secrecy regimes. Attribution may be
evident to authorized insiders, but is inaccessible to external analysts
without leaks, official summaries, or eventual declassification. Secrecy
is maintained through statute and security vetting, not exclusivity of
content.

### **Proprietary**

Access is gated by corporate or institutional control—paywalls,
subscription services, NDAs, or trade-secret protections. The material
is not secret under national law, but legal action (contract violation
or copyright infringement) can limit its redistribution.

### **Self-Declared**

The actor openly identifies itself or its sponsor— a ministry logo on a
press release, a political channel branded with a party’s name, or a
mercenary group named after its founder. The attribution is explicit and
part of the asset’s narrative identity.

### **Legally Documented**

Control is discoverable through public records such as court filings,
company registries, budget lines, procurement logs, or international
treaty annexes . The operator may present a different identity publicly,
but ownership is traceable through official documentation.

### **Technical Evidence**

While formal ownership is hidden, repeated use of
infrastructure—analytics IDs, certificates, IP blocks, TTPs, or leaked
credentials links an asset or operation to a specific actor. Attribution
requires no insider access but may demand technical investigation.

## Circumstantial or Deniable

These forms of attribution rely on inference. Signals may exist but lack
clarity or completeness. Attribution here is subject to debate,
variation in confidence levels, and plausible denial by the sponsor.

### **Inference**

The link between actor and asset emerges only through multi-source
fusion: technical forensics, behavioral signatures, HUMINT, and
contextual analysis. Analysts may reach high confidence, but public
deniability remains intact.

#### ***HUMINT (Human Intelligence)***

Insider accounts, recruited sources, or defectors provide direct but
unverifiable attribution. HUMINT can expose intent, structure, or
sponsorship, but its reliability, corroboration, and susceptibility to
manipulation vary.

#### ***Deductive Inference***

A conclusion derived from premises taken as accurate. If the premises
are true, the conclusion must be true. Used in legal reasoning and
structured analytic techniques when logical certainty is achievable.

#### ***Inductive Inference***

Generalization from repeated observation. Analysts infer patterns or
sponsorship from behavior, tactics, timing, or message consistency. The
outcome is probabilistic—confidence increases with the number and
coherence of observations.

#### ***Abductive Inference***

“Inference to the best explanation.” Given partial data, analysts
propose the most plausible sponsor. Common in attribution of
cyberattacks, information manipulation, or covert influence, where only
circumstantial indicators are available. Conclusions remain provisional.

## Other Types

### **False Flag**

The operator deliberately introduces misleading evidence—spoofed
infrastructure, stylized language, or fake affiliations—to deflect blame
or provoke misattribution. Detection often occurs only after technical
or forensic dissection.

### **Dormant Attribution**

Assets are pre-positioned without active sponsorship signatures.
Examples include shelved NGOs, inactive bots, or sleeper malware.
Attribution becomes possible only upon activation, when behavioral cues
or operational context reveal the presence of control.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Attribution exists along a spectrum. Concrete evidence enables direct
linkage through ownership, declaration, or forensic signature.
Circumstantial inference becomes necessary when signals are weak,
contradictory, or intentionally disguised. While both categories support
analytic judgment, they differ in legal strength, narrative authority,
and strategic risk.

# Signals

Signals are the fingerprints of an operation. They are the discrete,
externally visible artifacts—addresses, domains, hashes, tracking codes,
transaction patterns, and stylistic quirks—that betray otherwise hidden
architecture.

Unlike outcomes, which describe what an influence effort achieves,
signals offer a trail of breadcrumbs showing the mechanisms—how it
works, who runs it, and where it connects. An address posted on a mirror
site to accept cryptocurrency, a Google Analytics tag quietly reused
across “independent” outlets, or a malware hash published by a national
CERT—all sit in this category
[\[1–3\]](https://paperpile.com/c/0cgOyL/x65D+TL5G+R7Dt). Each one is
small, precise, and machine-readable, yet they outline the scaffolding
of a much larger ecosystem when assembled.

Technical signals dominate early discovery work. Domains such as
“sputnik.az” or “arabic.sputniknews.com” reveal network perimeters,
shared hosting, and naming conventions that tie ostensibly separate
properties to a single owner
[\[2,4,5\]](https://paperpile.com/c/0cgOyL/TL5G+Bk1gw+u0YwS). File
hashes like “5086989639aed17227b8d6b041ef3163” trace bespoke malware to
a specific build pipeline, linking new intrusions to past campaigns
attributed to the same unit. Tracking identifiers—Google Analytics,
Yandex Metrica, Facebook app IDs—bind clusters of websites together
through a common analytics backbone, even when the visual branding
differs. These artifacts permit high-confidence pivots: one domain
surfaces a shared certificate, the certificate exposes an IP block, the
block hosts dozens of additional sites, and an influence network
appears.

Financial signals expose the flow of resources that sustain operations
and incentivise collaborators. Blockchain addresses published by
sanctioned outlets to solicit donations demonstrate evasion tactics and
provide immutable records of incoming support. On-chain analysis can
trace contributions from known exchange wallets to those addresses,
illuminating donor geography or exchanges complicit in cash-outs.
Traditional banking signals—such as foundation endowments, shell-company
loans, and sudden spikes in NGO funding—achieve the same purpose in the
fiat space, though often with greater legal opacity. When mapped against
technical infrastructure, these financial markers sometimes reveal the
handoff between propaganda production and payment pipelines that reward
amplification.

Content signals sit inside the narratives themselves. Recurrent phrases,
identical paragraph structures, and synchronized visual templates allow
stylometric clustering even when authorship differs. Circular
citations—where a forged report originates in Russian, appears a week
later in English via a fringe “analysis” site, and is then quoted back
in Russian state media—leave a recursive trail of language reuse that
machine learning models flag with high confidence. Such signals confirm
coordination without relying on leaked documentation or whistleblowers;
the content’s DNA supplies the evidence.

Behavioral signals emerge from rhythm and timing. Botnets that post at
precise ten-minute intervals, influencer accounts that resurface after
synchronized dormancy, or petitions that explode with signatures in
regions lacking the population to support them—these patterns betray
artificial orchestration. Human operators slip too: activists returning
from training seminars often adopt the same slogans and protest
choreography across continents, leaving a behavioral watermark of their
shared preparation. Traffic spikes that coincide with televised segments
on state outlets mark the hand-off between broadcast and social
amplification.

The true power of signals unfolds when they converge. A single
cryptocurrency address may appear trivial until analysis links it to a
Telegram channel, which shares a domain that reuses a known analytics
tag. It hosts articles signed by an academic whose grant history follows
the same banking route highlighted in suspicious transfers. Each signal
enriches the others, allowing investigators to pivot rapidly,
corroborate attribution, forecast next moves, and harden defenses.

Signals, however, are not static. Operators adapt naming patterns,
rotate wallet addresses, and churn infrastructure to create noise.
Effective monitoring, therefore, demands continuous harvesting,
historical baselining, and cross-domain correlation. The landscape
rewards those who treat every hashtag and address not as trivia but as a
key, small, exact, and wholly sufficient for matching the machinery
behind the curtain.

# Planes

Influence does not operate in a vacuum. It travels through the
architecture of human society—across domains of emotion, belief,
infrastructure, law, and identity. We must disaggregate these domains
into planes to understand modern influence campaigns, particularly those
orchestrated by state and hybrid actors.

Each plane represents a distinct but permeable layer of the influenced
battlespace. These planes are neither arbitrary categories nor reducible
to simple disciplines like media, law, or psychology. Instead, they are
operational surfaces: dimensions of human behavior and structure that
can be mapped, targeted, and manipulated independently or in concert.

This schema of planes provides both tactical specificity and strategic
clarity. It helps planners identify attack surfaces, analysts detect
patterns, and defenders allocate resources. Most importantly, it shifts
influence analysis away from vague notions like “soft power” or “public
diplomacy” and toward a concrete understanding of where and how effects
are generated.

What follows is a delineation of ten distinct influence planes. Each is
defined by its core target (what it affects), its characteristic methods
(how it is influenced), and its intended outcomes (why it matters).
While no schema is exhaustive, this model captures the primary domains
through which contemporary adversaries seek to impose will below the
threshold of open warfare.

## Cognitive Plane

The cognitive plane is the domain of internal reasoning—how individuals
and groups interpret data, form judgments, and update beliefs. It is not
about what people know, but how they know it.

Influence operations in this plane target logic itself. They seed doubt,
generate contradiction, and exploit cognitive biases to erode
consistency. Rather than persuading people with a new idea, these
campaigns often aim to make belief formation impossible. By overwhelming
mental processing or reframing causality, they create interpretive
paralysis—a fog of understanding where all narratives seem equally
plausible or suspect.

Cognitive influence disables judgment.

## Psychological Plane

Distinct from cognition, the psychological plane targets the affective
core—how people feel, not think. It leverages emotion, instinct, and
trauma to provoke reactions: fear, disgust, euphoria, shame, despair.

The goal is to bypass rational resistance by saturating the audience
with affective signals. Operations in this plane might spread panic
through rumors, sap will through helplessness, or trigger rage through
outrage bait. Where the cognitive plane erodes the decision-making
process, the psychological plane seeks to hijack it.

Together, they create a powerful one-two punch: distortion of reason,
then domination of response.

## Cultural Plane

The cultural plane encompasses the collective frameworks that define
identity: values, norms, myths, and memory. It is the domain of “who we
are.”

Influence operations here do not simply target individuals, but groups.
They manipulate history, language, and symbolic meaning to fracture
unity or redirect allegiance. A cultural operation may invert a national
symbol, revise a historical grievance, or elevate a marginal tradition
to displace a dominant one.

The aim is to disrupt shared belonging. If cognitive operations make
truth uncertain, cultural operations make loyalty unstable.

## Spiritual or Moral Plane

Where culture answers who we are, the spiritual/moral plane addresses
why we fight.

This is the domain of sacred values, ethical principles, and religious
meaning. Influence operations in this plane reframe aggression as
virtue, cast adversaries as evil, or cloak political action in divine
sanction. When successfully activated, it is the most dangerous plane:
belief becomes absolute, compromise becomes heresy, and violence becomes
moral duty.

Radicalization often begins here—not with an idea or identity, but with
a perceived obligation to act in accordance with higher purpose.

## Information Plane

The information plane is the field of public knowledge: facts,
narratives, reporting, and rumor. It defines what is “known” within a
population or organization.

Influence in this plane is achieved by controlling visibility—what is
emphasized, omitted, reframed, or drowned in noise. Disinformation,
narrative warfare, and “firehose of falsehood” tactics overwhelm the
public’s ability to maintain consensus. The result is not just a
falsehood believed but a fractured reality.

Information operations rarely rely on a single lie. They win by making
truth harder to find and consensus impossible to sustain.

## Cyber Plane

The cyber plane comprises the digital infrastructure supporting
communication, data integrity, and decision systems. It is both a
delivery vector and an influence target.

Operations include hacking, spoofing, information exfiltration,
deepfakes, botnets, and synthetic amplification. While cyber
capabilities often enable attacks in other planes (leaking emails to
affect political perception), they are also used to disrupt or
manipulate trust in systems directly.

Cyber influence is not just about technology—it is about perceptions of
control, reliability, and reality in a digitized world.

## Legal Plane

The legal plane governs norms of obligation, jurisdiction, and process.
It comprises courts, treaties, enforcement mechanisms, and legitimacy
frameworks.

Influence here involves weaponizing the law itself. Lawfare campaigns
file strategic lawsuits, distort legal language, exploit procedural
loopholes, or create parallel legal authorities to challenge
adversaries’ legitimacy. These operations seek to slow decision-making,
reverse burdens of proof, or generate asymmetric legal constraints.

In the legal plane, coercion comes not through bullets but through
briefs. It is warfare by precedent, obstruction, and formal confusion.

## Economic or Financial Plane

The economic/financial plane targets resource distribution
systems—capital, trade, debt, energy. Influence in this domain
manipulates markets, weaponizes dependency, or creates material scarcity
to shift behavior.

This plane is especially potent in peacetime competition. Operations
include strategic investment, infrastructure entrapment, currency
manipulation, or corruption campaigns. The goal is to generate
compliance without confrontation—to make resistance more expensive than
accommodation.

Where other planes target perception or loyalty, the economic plane
targets feasibility.

## Relational Plane

The relational plane is the ecosystem of trust: interpersonal,
institutional, and intergovernmental relationships that shape access,
alignment, and action.

Influence involves infiltration, alliance manipulation, agent
recruitment, or elite capture. It is often invisible through informal
channels, patronage, or manufactured credibility. Strategic relational
influence enables all others—it creates the doors through which
cognitive, legal, or economic influence can enter.

This is the plane of diplomacy, betrayal, and backchannel sabotage.

## Kinetic or Physical Plane

The kinetic/physical plane represents the material environment—bodies,
terrain, infrastructure. It includes sabotage, demonstrations of force,
territorial seizure, and assassinations.

Though traditionally treated as separate from “influence,” kinetic
actions often serve strategic narrative and psychological goals. If
targeted and mediated correctly, a single act of violence can ripple
through multiple planes. Conversely, kinetic actions devoid of narrative
context may fail to produce the intended effect.

In the modern schema, kinetic tools are not excluded from influence—they
are its sharpest edge.

## Discussion

These ten planes provide a framework for understanding how influence
occurs and where it lands. They offer entry points for adversaries,
targets for defenders, and strategic depth for analysts.

Each plane is uniquely vulnerable, but its real power is combinatorial.
An operation that begins in the information plane may trigger cognitive
disruption, legal conflict, and psychological collapse. A cyber breach
may feed an economic crisis, while amplifying cultural grievances
through synthetic media.

To map influence is to map convergence.

In the next section, we explore why similar planes—such as cognitive,
psychological, cultural, and moral—must remain analytically distinct for
effective defense and response.

### **Why Similar Planes Remain Separate**

In the schema of influence planes, it is essential to recognize the
synergy among domains and the analytical necessity of keeping certain
planes distinct. While many overlap in practice, particularly in
multi-vector campaigns, distinctions in target, mechanism, and effect
warrant clear boundaries for operational clarity and effective
counterstrategy.

-   **Psychological vs. Cognitive:** These planes are often co-targeted
    but function through fundamentally different mechanisms. The
    psychological plane targets affective states—emotions, instincts,
    and trauma. It aims to shape how people *feel* and react under
    stress, using tools like fear induction, gaslighting, or awe. The
    effects are immediate and behavioral: mass panic, apathy, paralysis,
    or rage.

    The cognitive plane, in contrast, manipulates reasoning frameworks
    and belief systems. It governs how people *think*, interpret data,
    and conclude. Tools like information manipulation, heuristic
    exploitation, and bias triggers create confusion, contradiction, or
    logical dead ends. While psychological operations provoke visceral
    reactions, cognitive operations erode clarity, consistency, and
    rational agency. Separating the two helps planners recognize whether
    an operation distorts perception or destabilizes emotional
    resilience.

-   **Cultural vs. Spiritual/Moral:** These two planes influence
    collective identity and belonging but operate through distinct
    referents. Cultural operations manipulate shared norms, language,
    and historical memory to redefine group identities and reorient
    loyalties. Tools include mythmaking, symbolic inversion, or
    ethnopsychology. Strategic outcomes often involve fractured national
    cohesion or the substitution of one collective narrative for
    another.

    The spiritual/moral planes operate on a higher register of meaning.
    It leverages sacred values, religious narratives, and ethical frames
    to justify action, inspire zealotry, or delegitimize opposition.
    Operations in this plane might co-opt clergy, invoke divine
    sanction, or invert moral frameworks. Whereas culture answers *who
    we are*, the spiritual and moral domains answer *why we fight*. The
    distinction is critical when analyzing mobilization around
    existential or transcendental causes.

-   **Legal vs. Information:** Both planes involve perception of
    legitimacy, but one is rooted in formal structures, the other in
    informal consensus. The legal plane targets judicial systems,
    governance norms, and international law through lawfare, regulatory
    manipulation, and treaty sabotage. It aims to constrain adversaries,
    create asymmetric obligations, or exploit procedural blind spots.

    The information plane, by contrast, shapes what is accepted as
    accurate. Through propaganda, narrative control, selective release,
    and the “firehose of falsehood” tactic, it fragments shared reality
    and undermines public trust. When used together, legal and
    informational tactics can launder manipulated information into
    perceived legitimacy—by embedding false narratives into legal
    proceedings or court records.

-   **Kinetic and Physical:** The kinetic/physical plane is conceptually
    separate, not because it is unrelated, but because its
    methods—violence, sabotage, territorial control—are often considered
    outside the scope of malign influence. Yet, modern hybrid doctrine
    intentionally blurs this boundary, creating ambiguity about the
    appropriate response. A kinetic act can have psychological,
    informational, and relational effects by design (an assassination
    live-streamed to terrorize or motivate).

    Kinetic tools should not be siloed. They must be understood as a
    part of the continuum of influence.

-   **Planes as Modular but Interdependent:** Each plane in the schema
    provides a targetable domain of human function or social structure,
    allowing for a modular yet interdependent approach. What makes the
    schema powerful is not that each plane can be attacked in isolation,
    but that they form combinatorial opportunities. For example:

    -   A relational breach (agent recruitment) can facilitate cognitive
        operations (targeted disinfo).

    -   A cyber intrusion can enable economic disruption or narrative
        warfare by releasing leaked documents.

    -   A cultural narrative seeded through media can evolve into
        spiritual radicalization or legal confrontation.

The schema, therefore, encourages planners to see not just targets, but
entry points into interlinked systems—allowing for cascading influence,
asymmetric leverage, and layered ambiguity.

Distinguishing between these planes is more than a theoretical exercise.
It is a practical imperative. Each plane offers its vulnerabilities,
tools, and defense requirements. Separating them enables more accurate
threat modeling, targeted countermeasures, and agile operational design.
As adversaries exploit the seams between these domains, understanding
the planes independently and as a system is essential to defending
cognitive security, societal resilience, and geopolitical sovereignty.
