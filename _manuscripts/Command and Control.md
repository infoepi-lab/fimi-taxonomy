# Overview {#overview}

Command and control in authoritarian systems is not confined to military
hierarchies or emergency protocols. It extends through institutions
often treated as distinct—intelligence, media, diplomacy, and private
enterprise—that, in practice, function as interdependent components of a
single influence architecture. The instruments vary, but the outcome is
consistent: centralized decision-making, managed perception, and the
suppression of alternatives.

This section outlines the core components of that architecture. It
begins with the regime itself—not as a solitary ruler or party
apparatus, but as a network of coercive institutions, loyal elites, and
doctrinal imperatives. From there, it traces how control is
operationalized through intelligence services, diplomatic channels,
state and oligarchic media, and proxy economic networks. It also
examines the deliberate targeting of journalists, researchers, and other
information-gathering actors—both domestically and abroad—not only to
punish dissent but to prevent the production of conflicting knowledge.

These instruments do not operate in isolation. They reinforce one
another. Intelligence agencies shape the narratives that state media
promotes. Diplomats echo talking points seeded by front organizations.
Mercenary companies project force while masking the chain of command.
The structure is modular by design, built for flexibility, redundancy,
and plausible deniability.

What emerges is not a singular doctrine but a reproducible pattern:
systems of control engineered to thrive under ambiguity, to blur the
line between policy and propaganda, and to render opposition not only
dangerous but difficult to discern.

*Command and Control* refers to the mechanisms through which governments
and other powerful entities establish authority and shape
decision-making structures. These include:

-   *Repression*, as defined by ACLED—the use of violence by state
    forces against civilians or protesters—to enforce information
    discipline and deter dissent.
    -   *Targeting of information gatherers*, such as journalists,
        scientists, and academics, to suppress independent reporting and
        enforce conformity with official narratives.\
-   *Extrajudicial killings*, targeted executions carried out by state
    agents or proxies to eliminate rivals or intimidate potential
    challengers.\
-   *Economic coercion*, where financial leverage is used to induce
    compliance, punish dissent, or extract concessions from foreign
    governments.\
-   *Diplomatic channels*, which advance influence operations under the
    guise of policy dialogue or multilateral cooperation.\
-   *Kinetic military action*, where armed forces are deployed to seize
    territory, eliminate opposition, or impose regime objectives through
    direct violence.\
-   *Oligarchic media*, privately held outlets aligned with state
    interests that shape perception while maintaining a façade of
    independence.\
-   *Domestic intelligence operations*, the continuous surveillance and
    neutralization of internal threats to maintain regime stability.\
-   *Support for autocratic leaders*, the export of material, political,
    and informational resources to sustain allied regimes abroad.

Together, these mechanisms consolidate control, remove independent
sources of interpretation or resistance, and structure the information
environment to ensure that only state-sanctioned narratives are visible,
credible, or safe to repeat.

# Varieties {#varieties}

## The Regime {#the-regime}

The regime sits at the center of command and control, not just as
leadership, but as infrastructure. It links political authority,
intelligence services, military institutions, and oligarch-aligned
capital into a system that governs through coordination more than
command. In hybrid influence systems, the regime is less a person or
office than a network: overlapping elites, coercive actors, and shared
goals that outlast any individual.

Its role is not symbolic. It sets parameters for acceptable speech,
assigns threat value, selects targets, and distributes both force and
narrative. That influence is exerted through ministries, informal
councils, and patronage networks alike. What matters is not the form but
the function: shaping tempo, synchronizing action, and keeping other
instruments aligned.

The regime does not need to speak for its priorities to register. They
show up in state media headlines, in the timing of arrests, in the
silences that follow unexplained deaths. Without that central structure,
the rest of the system fragments. With it, disparate actions take on the
shape of strategy.

-   *The Kremlin’s Vertical of Power (Russia, 2000–present):* Vladimir
    Putin’s consolidation of the *siloviki* (security elites)
    illustrates regime-as-infrastructure. Senior figures circulate
    between intelligence agencies, state corporations, and ministries,
    maintaining coordination through personal loyalty and shared
    enrichment. The *presidential administration* acts as a nerve center
    linking propaganda (RT, TASS), intelligence (FSB, SVR, GRU), and
    oligarchic finance (Rosneft, Gazprom, VTB). The result is not
    command by decree but synchronized reaction—media, diplomacy, and
    paramilitary activity aligning automatically to regime priorities.\
    *The Chinese Party-State (CCP under Xi Jinping):* The *Leading Small
    Groups* (领导小组) system institutionalizes coordination across
    ministries and sectors. These cross-cutting committees link
    propaganda, cyber governance, foreign affairs, and military
    modernization under direct Party supervision. The Central Cyberspace
    Affairs Commission, chaired by Xi, exemplifies this blending of
    political, technical, and ideological authority—ensuring that online
    discourse, tech policy, and security operations move in lockstep.

-   *Iran’s Deep State (IRGC and Clerical Oversight):* Iran’s Islamic
    Revolutionary Guard Corps operates as both a military entity and an
    economic-political conglomerate, controlling construction,
    telecommunications, and media through front companies. It
    coordinates coercion, patronage, and information operations on
    behalf of the Supreme Leader, binding formal governance to informal
    control networks.

-   *North Korea’s “Monolithic Ideological System”:* The *Organization
    and Guidance Department* functions as the regime’s internal control
    grid—supervising the military, Party, media, and even cultural life.
    It ensures that all institutions echo central messaging without
    requiring constant top-down orders.

-   *Post-Soviet Hybrid Coordination (Belarus and Serbia):* Belarus
    under Lukashenko and Serbia under Vučić both demonstrate how regimes
    synchronize state institutions and business oligarchs through
    informal councils. Key media outlets receive privileged access and
    funding in exchange for editorial compliance, while security
    services coordinate with pro-regime business networks to manage
    dissent and elections. The effect is systemic alignment without
    visible repression.

-   *Turkey’s “Coordination Councils” and Crony Networks:* Erdoğan’s use
    of the *Presidency of Communications* and the *Wealth Fund* mirrors
    a soft form of command-and-control—consolidating narrative power,
    financial leverage, and security coordination under a single
    executive umbrella, while maintaining the appearance of pluralism
    through party and business intermediaries.

These examples reveal the regime not as a solitary ruler but as a
synchronized ecosystem of elites, agencies, and institutions—governing
through alignment rather than instruction, where shared incentives and
enforced loyalty replace formal hierarchy.

## Intelligence Agencies {#intelligence-agencies}

Intelligence agencies in hybrid-authoritarian systems do more than
collect information. They function as primary instruments of narrative
enforcement and operational execution. Under Soviet doctrine—which still
shapes the practices of post-Soviet regimes like Russia—intelligence
services are not external observers but integral components of the
command system, built to shape reality as much as document it
[\[1,2\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/unjNc+Gmklp).

Entities like the KGB—and its successors, including the FSB, SVR, and
GRU—operate beyond bureaucratic bounds
[\[3\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/eqVTf). They are embedded across
the military, diplomatic, economic, and informational sectors. Their
presence forms a layered mesh of overlapping jurisdictions and shared
objectives. Intelligence is not a discrete structure here. It’s the
connective tissue—linking, reinforcing, and extending the regime’s
reach. The razvedka architecture of the Soviet period remains the
clearest example of this approach
[\[4\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/1HFmL).

## Diplomatic Channels {#diplomatic-channels}

Diplomatic channels—formal or informal—are tools for negotiation,
coordination, and influence between states. While often framed as
mechanisms for legitimate cooperation, they frequently carry strategic
ambiguity. Treaties and agreements may include vague clauses, such as
references to “military cooperation,” that can later be invoked to
justify intervention or escalation. These channels operate across legal
and cultural domains, allowing the regime to shape perception, build
influence among local elites, and construct a veneer of legitimacy for
future actions.

Diplomatic channels can also be used as little more than a megaphone for
state media [\[5\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/JaJSE). False claims
made during meetings of international bodies are one example.

-   Russia has derailed nonproliferation meetings related to biological
    weapons by making claims about bioweapons development in Ukraine, a
    long-standing false claim that traces back to a GRU front
    [\[5–7\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/JaJSE+9JFTY+2dQWU).

-   Russia has invited bloggers who promoted false claims about the
    White Helmets in Syria to the UN
    [\[8\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/IovQp)

## Overt State Media {#overt-state-media}

Overt state media—such as Pravda, TASS, or Russia Today—serve as a
central broadcasting arm of regime authority
[\[9,10\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/R0I1n+axMfT). These outlets
function less as journalism, though they go to great lengths to mimic
the appearance and language of credible media outlets, and more as
ideological enforcement mechanisms. They deliver approved narratives,
manufacture crises, obscure dissent, and frame the regime’s actions as
defensive, moral, or inevitable.

Because their visibility is explicit, these media do not aim to deceive
by hiding their allegiance. Instead, they project dominance, showing
that the regime controls the truth and does not need to apologize.

In the information plane, overt media is a key tool for setting
discourse boundaries, drowning out rival narratives, and forcing
adversaries into a reactive posture. It also acts as a signal amplifier,
cueing aligned outlets, influencers, and proxies on messaging
priorities.

-   The editor-in-chief of RT has repeatedly described the role of state
    media as critical as defense. She has compared state funding for RT
    to defense, saying that you spend on both for the same reason
    [\[11\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/5AWeS).

-   Outlets like TASS are critical in broadcasting key claims and
    lending them legitimacy. Although TASS has published unsubstantiated
    claims about the US's development of biological weapons, until 2022,
    it had a partnership with Reuters, a highly respected and generally
    credible news source
    [\[12–15\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/zblXv+VoJ1s+Re9ST+oh20s).

## Oligarchic Media {#oligarchic-media}

Parallel to state media, oligarchic media form a distributed layer of
regime-aligned influence with greater flexibility and reduced
attribution. Outlets like Tsargrad TV or think tanks like Katehon serve
ideological goals while preserving the appearance of independence.

These platforms are often funded by Kremlin-friendly oligarchs and
staffed by nationalist intellectuals, ultra-conservative clerics, or
information manipulation veterans. Their role is to inject
regime-aligned narratives into broader cultural and political debates,
particularly in foreign environments where overt state branding would
raise suspicion.

Oligarchic media operates in the information and financial planes,
allowing command functions to spread organically through loyalty,
patronage, and ideology networks.

### **Example in Practice: Malofeev's Media Network** {#example-in-practice:-malofeev's-media-network}

Konstantin Malofeev, sanctioned by the U.S. and EU, funds Tsargrad and
Katehon, and maintains relationships with pro-Russian religious and
paramilitary groups. His outlets:

-   Promote specific Orthodox religious views\
-   Demonize minority groups\
-   Promote Russian exceptionalism and civilizational conflict.\
-   Justify armed conflict in Ukraine, Syria, and the Balkans\
-   Support “Russkiy Mir” ideology, advancing the idea of a unified
    cultural-political space across post-Soviet and Slavic territories

These operations contribute to softening foreign resistance, justifying
military action, and maintaining coherence among ideological hardliners
within Russia and its sphere of influence.

## Targeting Information Gatherers {#targeting-information-gatherers}

According to the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED),
*repression* refers to the use of violence by state forces against
civilians or protesters—a measurable indicator of how governments
respond to dissent and social disorder
[\[19\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/L9Dv). Within Infomap, this
concept extends beyond physical coercion to encompass the full
architecture of control that governs who may speak, research, publish,
or criticize.

Repression is not only what happens in the streets but also what happens
to knowledge itself: the regulation of interpretation through fear,
legal constraint, and ambient threat. It is the functional mechanism
through which regimes maintain information discipline, converting
uncertainty into obedience.

Regimes conduct surveillance through informant networks, threaten
dissidents, force them into exile, hold their relatives hostage at home,
and, in some cases, harm dissidents directly
[\[20–22\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/Rxf8+Gect+2A9l). These
tactics instilled fear and mistrust among co-nationals, depoliticized
their speech and social life, and rendered anti-regime activism a
high-risk activity.

Across all of these domains—domestic and foreign—the targeting of
epistemic actors is becoming increasingly anonymized, decentralized, and
challenging to trace. It is no longer a matter of direct censorship, but
of systemic constraint on what may be known, said, or trusted. The
result is not silence in the traditional sense, but the managed
domestication of discourse itself.

### **DOMESTIC** {#domestic}

True command and control is impossible without information discipline.
Authoritarian regimes must not only generate narratives but also prevent
unauthorized stories from gaining traction. To achieve this, they
systematically target journalists, academics, scientists, some clergy,
whistleblowers, and others whose work contributes to building knowledge
and may conflict with official positions.

This targeting achieves multiple aims. It suppresses dissent, deters
through fear, signals to broader society, and manipulates collective
memory. The suppression occurs through direct coercion and what might be
called ambient repression—forms of threat that do not require
centralized enforcement, because the population has already internalized
the risk.

Self-censorship is one example of this, representing what historian
Timothy Snyder calls “anticipatory obedience”—when individuals
anticipate what a more repressive government might want and then offer
themselves without being asked.

-   Surveillance and harassment are used to induce a continuous sense of
    fear. Individuals are monitored online and offline, often without
    formal charges, cultivating a sense of an ever-watchful government
    eye. Recent revelations show governments using spyware against civil
    society and media, with numerous states revealed to be targeting
    activists and journalists with sophisticated surveillance
    technology, much of it supplied by companies with close military
    links. This creates what researchers call a “chilling climate” where
    both journalists and sources experience “paranoia” as a tactic with
    longstanding precedent used by intelligence agencies to pressure
    activists and journalists.

-   Strategic lawsuits and criminal charges are deployed to tie
    dissenters down in legal or financial precarity. These Strategic
    Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPPs) have become an
    all-too-common tool for intimidating and silencing criticism through
    expensive, baseless legal proceedings
    [\[23–25\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/TdvT+Oyn3+ITkY). From
    Russia to Kyrgyzstan and Georgia to Hungary, “foreign agent” style
    laws have become a preferred instrument for authoritarians to
    extinguish critical voices, shield their rule from scrutiny, and
    strengthen their hold on power
    [\[26\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/xKzH). The claimants aim to
    silence critical reporting about them, using whatever law—criminal
    or civil—is most convenient. The burden of defending a case means
    that merely threatening litigation can be enough for a media outlet
    to withdraw a report or issue an apology.

-   Discrediting campaigns are launched to destroy reputations rather
    than refute ideas. This includes media smear pieces, selective data
    leaks, manipulated digital forensics, and broader efforts to recast
    critics as morally or politically suspect
    [\[27–30\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/cEXG+N0sy+0HHr+TxnN).
    Digital threats like cyberattacks, surveillance, doxing, and
    harassment affect journalists' ability to reach their audiences. At
    the same time, smear campaigns undermine their credibility, and the
    intimidation of family members exacts a high psychological toll.

-   Physical intimidation includes actions such as arbitrary arrests,
    police raids, disappearances, and indefinite pre-trial detention
    [\[31–33\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/egLj+Sub1+qRf7).
    Authoritarian campaigns to clamp down on dissent and control the
    media have driven scores of journalists from countries like
    Afghanistan, Belarus, Hong Kong, Myanmar, Nicaragua, and Russia into
    exile. However, the repression of a free press does not stop at the
    border. Freedom House has identified 112 acts of transnational
    repression committed against journalists by 26 governments between
    2014 and 2023, including physical attacks, unlawful deportations,
    detentions, renditions, digital harassment, and reprisals against
    family members.

-   Infrastructural throttling occurs when the regime dismantles the
    support systems that enable independent work. This includes revoking
    licenses, dissolving NGOs, banning publications, or denying research
    funding and academic appointments
    [\[34\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/u4XV). In Russia, for
    example, the invasion of Ukraine led to new legislation restricting
    press freedom, with laws punishing the spreading of
    non-Kremlin-approved information with up to 15 years in prison,
    making covering the war practically illegal for Russian reporters
    [\[35,36\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/6pTH+5efr).

-   Digital interference targets a person's virtual footprint. It
    includes account suspensions, algorithmic downranking, spyware
    targeting, and the spread of deepfakes—tools that erode credibility
    or silence voices by degrading digital access or polluting
    reputational space
    [\[37–39\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/FTIp+PELN+Vc57). As more
    countries move toward authoritarianism and technological advances
    proliferate, the authoritarian digital arsenal continues to expand
    and diversify, encompassing geopolitical tensions and the rapid
    growth of generative artificial intelligence.

The purpose of these measures is not simply censorship. It is to instill
anticipatory obedience—a condition where individuals self-censor, not
because they are explicitly silenced, but because they learn to
calculate risk and act accordingly
[\[40\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/Iw0v). Knowledge, in such an
environment, becomes a contested and dangerous asset.

### **ABROAD** {#abroad}

This logic of targeting does not stop at national borders. Instead, it
is projected outward to suppress dissent among diaspora communities,
discredit adversarial narratives, and intimidate foreign actors from
engaging critically. What appear to be isolated incidents when viewed
separately—an assassination here, a kidnapping there—in fact form a
constant threat across the world that is affecting the lives of millions
of people and changing how activists, journalists, and regular
individuals go about their lives. By 2024, some 44 countries have been
documented as committing transnational repression, according to Freedom
House [\[41\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/9T6E).

-   Visa denials and travel bans are applied to investigative
    journalists, scholars, and activists, denying them access to forums
    where they might share critical findings or collaborate across
    borders. At least 55 governments worldwide employ one or more
    mobility control methods to restrict freedom of movement, including
    revoking citizenship, passport restrictions, denial of consular
    services, and travel bans, to coerce and punish dissidents,
    activists, journalists, and ordinary people
    [\[41\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/9T6E). Since 2019, Amnesty
    International has documented the cases of at least six Kashmiri
    journalists, human rights activists, academics, and politicians who
    have been barred from travelling outside India without any lawful
    justification [\[42\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/VAqS). These
    restrictions hinder people's ability to express dissent and
    participate in pro-democracy activism, signaling to potential
    government critics that they may face similar consequences.

-   Digital intimidation campaigns are conducted through troll armies,
    doxing, and orchestrated reporting aimed at deplatforming or
    silencing critics. A 2017 report from Freedom House indicated that
    30 governments worldwide paid keyboard armies to spread propaganda
    and attack critics, using paid commentators, trolls, and bots to
    harass journalists and erode trust in the media
    [\[43\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/GFpA). The efforts have
    succeeded often, sending journalists into exile or effectively
    silencing online expression. While a DDoS attack targets hardware,
    doxxing is far more personal because it targets a person's private
    life, revealing someone's identity or whereabouts as a common tactic
    to intimidate
    [\[44–47\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/SSnW+pVjM+zQAg+dfPE).
    These actions regularly blur the line between cyberattack and
    psychological operation.

-   Extraterritorial surveillance happens through embassies, cultural
    centers, or compromised platforms. Governments also rely on passport
    cancellations and international arrest warrants to trap and detain
    opponents abroad. At the same time, digital technologies provide
    authoritarian rulers with powerful tools to extend surveillance and
    control beyond borders and deep into the personal lives of exiles.
    Surveillance may serve as an instrument of controlling “voice”
    abroad in multiple ways: mirroring extensive repression within
    authoritarian states, governments design surveillance systems to
    monitor diaspora communities
    [\[1,22,36,48,49\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/unjNc+5efr+p3jk+2A9l+6gI8).
    Digital technologies have given authoritarian governments new tools
    to control, silence, and punish dissent across borders, enabling
    regimes to monitor and respond to the activities of political exiles
    and diaspora communities with greater scope and speed.

-   Proxy litigation is used to entangle critics in lawsuits or criminal
    proceedings in third-party countries with pliable legal systems.
    States use domestic forms of repression as a means of punishing,
    threatening, or controlling those who reside overseas through
    strategies that involve long-distance coercion-by-proxy. These cases
    are often filed under pretexts, such as incitement or hate speech,
    creating a chilling effect without requiring a win in the case. This
    can be considered a “low cost” form of transnational repression, as
    it neither violates the sovereignty of other states nor is likely to
    garner significant levels of diplomatic or media attention.

-   Funding disruption is a method of strangulation. Authoritarian
    regimes have found more sophisticated ways to repress their
    opponents, using FATF regulations to justify the confiscation of
    assets and freezing of hundreds of millions belonging to churches,
    universities, and dissidents. Donors are pressured, financial access
    is revoked, and NGOs are labeled extremist or subversive, thereby
    blocking their ability to function. Authoritarians manipulate the
    FATF's forty recommendations for stopping money laundering and
    terrorist financing to justify financial repression, closing
    thousands of civil society organizations and expropriating their
    assets.

-   Narrative laundering is carried out through third-party
    actors—foreign academics, think tanks, journalists—who are invited
    to write op-eds, join panels, or contribute to reports that subtly
    echo regime-aligned narratives while casting doubt on opposition
    voices
    [\[50–53\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/pcVCN+ECpU+Lhw6+RH9Y).
    Narrative laundering—the technique of transferring a specific
    narrative from its state-run origins into the broader media
    ecosystem through aligned publications, "useful idiots," and
    possibly witting participants—is an "active-measures" tactic that
    has been adapted for the social-media era
    [\[9,54–57\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/GQiw+R0I1n+mHb6+O0X7+IFJl).
    Authoritarian think tanks operate much like their democratic
    counterparts—organizing public conferences and events, publishing
    research in academic journals and on their websites, and sharing
    analyses with media outlets. But the antiliberal and antidemocratic
    political systems to which these entities belong repress dissent and
    claim control over the discursive and ideational space.

-   Third-party influence is also deployed through aligned diaspora
    members, dual nationals, or regional partners who act as surrogate
    messengers. The recent revelation concerning a US–Belgian citizen,
    who had not set foot in Rwanda since she left as a small child,
    being targeted by the Rwandan government, illustrated with shocking
    clarity the long reach of authoritarian regimes. Their cultural
    proximity makes their critiques of dissenters appear more credible
    and “authentic” to local audiences. Post-Soviet oligarchs connected
    to interference efforts have donated between \$372 million and \$435
    million to more than 200 institutions, with the gifts appearing to
    follow two specific threads: establishing a potential toehold over
    policy decisions and so-called reputation laundering
    [\[58\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/MlaD).

## Oligarchic Mercenary Companies {#oligarchic-mercenary-companies}

Oligarchic mercenary companies such as the Wagner Group represent a
sophisticated evolution of state violence: a fusion of military
coercion, commercial exploitation, and narrative warfare—all under the
veil of deniability. These organizations function not as rogue actors
but as extralegal arms of the regime, used to project power, maintain
plausible deniability, and entrench influence across global theaters.

Though labeled a “private military company” (PMC), Wagner has never
operated independently
[\[59–61\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/vmOc6+keHWm+Asnz3). Ex-GRU
commander Dmitry Utkin founded the organization, which was effectively
run by the Russian oligarch Yevgeny Prigozhin, whose tight relationships
with Russian intelligence and defense leaders have been extensively
documented [\[62\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/lgKzL).

Investigations have revealed that Wagner operatives were trained at
facilities adjacent to the GRU in Molkyno, and that Prigozhin maintained
regular contact with senior figures, including Defense Minister Sergei
Shoigu and GRU General Alexey Dyumin. Wagner's command structure thus
lies within the orbit of Russian military intelligence, and its
operational freedom depends on state protection
[\[63,64\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/8dHZO+vTzNx).

Despite its nominal illegality—private military companies remain neither
fully legal nor banned under Russian law—Wagner was heavily deployed by
the Russian state in Crimea, Donbas, Syria, Libya, Sudan, the Central
African Republic (CAR), and Mali
[\[65–71\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/dffZw+JJcVb+OuoaK+f3A1D+3ulcK+hOpCo+WvY1k).

Wagner performed state-like functions in many regions: training foreign
forces, managing VIP protection, conducting election influence
campaigns, extracting resources (notably gold, oil, and rare minerals),
and targeting journalists. In CAR alone, Wagner operatives have been
implicated in murders of investigative reporters, UN-targeted attacks,
and systematic extrajudicial executions
[\[72,73\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/gznEp+CCoLD).

The company also oversaw front entities, such as AFRIC (Association for
Free Research and International Cooperation) and Africa Back Office,
which were tasked with developing and deploying influence
strategies—combining military intimidation with politically driven
propaganda, economic infiltration, and ethnographic manipulation
[\[50,74,75\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/pcVCN+eiQhu+8FNnp). These
units coordinated directly with Russia’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs and
Ministry of Defense, collapsing the public-private divide.

Oligarchic mercenary companies like Wagner operate in both the physical
and cognitive planes, while helping a regime specifically avoid things
like diplomatic recognition:

-   Physically, they act as an irregular army, capable of force
    projection, regime protection, and deterrence
    [\[17,66,74,76\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/JJcVb+nDf83+eiQhu+9gqa).

-   Cognitively, they instill fear, signal regime reach, suppress
    journalism, and provide “muscle” for hybrid influence campaigns
    [\[74,77,78\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/eiQhu+iObD+ovPY).

Their utility lies in strategic ambiguity. Wagner can be activated
globally without triggering formal war thresholds or diplomatic
consequences. When caught, it is disavowed. When successful, it reshapes
realities in favor of Russian interests—militarily, economically, and
ideologically.

Even after the failed 2023 mutiny and the apparent death of Prigozhin,
Wagner’s model has persisted. Reports suggest that its activities are
fragmented into successor companies, such as RPOST-M LLC in Syria, which
continue operations under new names while preserving structural
continuity [\[79\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/ijFuP). This modular
restructuring allows the Kremlin to maintain the strategic value of
coercive proxy forces while diffusing liability and reasserting control.

In this way, oligarchic mercenary companies are not merely battlefield
tools. They are command-and-control extensions of the regime itself,
carrying out geopolitically sensitive missions that no formal
institution could claim—and no adversary can easily counter
[\[50,61,73,80,81\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/Asnz3+pcVCN+jl35+CCoLD+52XE).

## Kinetic Military Action {#kinetic-military-action}

While many command mechanisms are subtle or indirect, conventional
military action remains a central pillar of military strategy. It is the
most explicit tool of regime enforcement—used when narrative warfare,
proxies, and diplomacy have failed or are insufficient.

-   Historical examples such as the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia and
    the attempted three-day takeover of Ukraine in 2022 show that
    kinetic action is used to decapitate rival leadership, replace
    governments, or physically reassert strategic dominance
    [\[82,83\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/hAGry+oHvfB).

These operations are choreographed to neutralize command nodes, control
critical infrastructure, and prevent organized resistance. Although
overt and high-risk, kinetic operations generate shockwaves across all
planes; it is the only action that does so. But this comes at a high
cost.

## Extrajudicial Killings {#extrajudicial-killings}

Extrajudicial killings are among the most chilling instruments of
command and control. These are precision assassinations of defectors,
dissidents, journalists, intelligence insiders, or anyone who has seen
or known too much. These killings often take place well beyond the
regime’s territorial borders. Their aim is both tactical and
psychological.

These acts are deliberately narrow in scope but enormous in symbolic
weight. A single hit, staged so that attribution is obvious but
deniable, can have the impact of a thousand arrests. The assassinations
of Alexander Litvinenko (poisoned with polonium-210 in London, 2006),
Anna Politkovskaya (shot outside her Moscow apartment in 2006), and
Sergei Skripal (targeted with Novichok nerve agent in the UK, 2018) are
a few examples. In each case, someone was challenging the regime’s
ability to dictate the truth
[\[84–86\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/GPpRf+hdZmV+00now).

These operations are covert in planning and conspicuous in effect.
Exotic poisons, symbolic timing, and signature methods are not
accidents—they are semiotic. Politkovskaya was murdered on Putin’s
birthday [\[84\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/GPpRf). The nerve agent
used against Skripal could only have come from Russia. The goal is not
merely to kill, but to choreograph fear.

Functionally, extrajudicial killings operate across three planes:

-   *Physical*: eliminating strategic threats.\
-   *Cognitive*: sowing fear and uncertainty.\
-   *Relational*: signaling betrayal carries irrevocable cost.

The message is consistent: “No one is beyond reach. Loyalty is
permanent. Betrayal is fatal.” These killings serve not only to erase
individuals but to extinguish information, fracture opposition, and
dissuade defection
[\[87–90\]](https://paperpile.com/c/BmhEx6/ch30+E8Lm+q89A+h0tT). In
modern command architecture, extrajudicial assassination is enforcement,
narrative control, and elite discipline—made lethal by design.

# Discussion {#discussion}

Command and control is not a doctrine of constant direction but a
condition of organized obedience. It describes how power moves through
systems—how the regime maintains coherence across actors who may never
receive explicit orders—some may even partake in the structure without
understanding who their work ultimately serves.

Coordination replaces command; expectation replaces instruction. The
objective is not simply to issue directives but to create an environment
in which the correct decisions are made automatically. Control functions
through habit and hierarchy. Intelligence services interpret, rather
than invent, the regime’s will. Media internalize the boundaries of the
permissible and calibrate their content accordingly. Oligarchs
anticipate the preferences of power when allocating funding or acquiring
assets. Military and paramilitary structures are synchronized not
through continual supervision but through shared incentives and
ideological framing. Each domain reflects a single logic: to act as
though the center were always watching.

The efficiency of command and control lies in its ability to distribute
responsibility without diluting authority. It allows for both discipline
and deniability—discipline because outcomes remain predictable,
deniability because no one actor can be traced as the explicit origin of
direction. In practice, it fuses bureaucratic control with informal
patronage, ensuring that every lever of power—legal, informational, and
coercive—responds to the same gravitational pull.

### **Command and Control ≠ Centralization** {#≠-centralization}

Command and control is often mistaken for centralization, but the two
are not synonymous. Centralization concentrates power in a single node;
command and control diffuses it through calibrated networks that can act
semi-independently while remaining ideologically synchronized. The
regime does not need to issue a command for compliance to occur. It
governs through alignment—by defining the parameters of loyalty and risk
so precisely that even autonomous actors reinforce central priorities.

This is why hybrid regimes appear chaotic but remain coherent: they
tolerate rhetorical pluralism and operational improvisation, yet every
deviation occurs within a range that sustains the system’s stability.
The appearance of spontaneity conceals structured conformity.

### **Informational Feedback Loops** {#informational-feedback-loops}

The command structure depends on information flow as much as on
discipline. Intelligence agencies, media systems, and diplomatic
networks create feedback loops that inform the regime’s perception of
reality. Yet these loops are fragile, distorted by the very obedience
they cultivate. Reports are filtered to confirm expectations; state
media reflect only what superiors wish to see. The system becomes both
omniscient and blind—possessing enormous data collection capacity but
minimal epistemic diversity.

This self-reinforcing cycle of perception and action produces a closed
cognitive environment. Decisions are justified by the information the
system itself generates, and dissenting data are reclassified as a
threat. The result is stability without correction—an architecture that
functions smoothly until it fails catastrophically.
