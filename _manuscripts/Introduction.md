# **Introduction**

Infomap is a modular, interoperable system for tracking influence within
complex information ecosystems. Rather than asking only who is behind an
operation, Infomap is designed to surface what is being done, how, and
to what effect. It maps out the functions of influence and tracks how
they interact across media, digital platforms, political processes,
legal landscapes, economic incentives, and cultural norms. This
function-based architecture supports the analysis of both coordinated
campaigns and emergent phenomena, offering a scalable approach to
diagnose, track, and respond to influence operations in real-time. It
could be integrated with existing frameworks such as MISP (Malware
Information Sharing Platform) or the DISARM Framework, enhancing their
analytical capabilities while maintaining interoperability with
established threat intelligence workflows.

## **The Function-Based Approach** {#the-function-based-approach}

Function in information manipulation refers to the strategic role that
an activity, asset, or behavior plays within a broader influence
architecture. Rather than focusing solely on tactics, tools, or stated
intent, function classifies elements based on their systemic
position—specifically, how they support, maintain, or challenge existing
power structures and information flows. This analytical lens reveals
patterns that transcend individual actors, platforms, or campaigns,
enabling more robust detection and response mechanisms.

Functions operate at multiple scales simultaneously. A single tweet
might serve a legitimation function within a domestic political campaign
while simultaneously functioning as operational multiplication for a
foreign influence network. This multi-layered nature means that analysts
must consider not only the immediate context of an information object,
but also its broader systemic role across different audiences,
timeframes, and operational environments.

Functions can be categorized into several structural roles, each serving
distinct purposes within influence architectures:

-   **Command and Control**: Activities that coordinate, direct, or
    synchronize influence operations across multiple actors, platforms,
    or domains. This includes both hierarchical coordination (top-down
    directives) and distributed coordination (peer-to-peer
    synchronization through shared narratives or timing).

-   **Legitimation**: Efforts to establish credibility, authority, or
    moral justification for particular narratives, actors, or actions.
    This function often involves the cultivation of authentic-seeming
    voices, the appropriation of legitimate institutions' credibility,
    or the construction of pseudo-expert networks.

-   **Obfuscation**: Activities designed to obscure true sources,
    methods, or intentions while maintaining plausible deniability. This
    includes not only technical obfuscation (such as sockpuppet
    networks) but also conceptual obfuscation (such as flooding
    information spaces with contradictory narratives).

-   **Sabotage**: Direct attacks on information integrity, institutional
    credibility, or social cohesion. Unlike other functions that seek to
    build alternative narratives, sabotage primarily aims to destroy
    trust in existing systems without necessarily offering replacements.

-   **Operational Multiplication**: Mechanisms that amplify the reach,
    frequency, or apparent authenticity of influence operations. This
    includes both technical multiplication (bot networks, coordinated
    sharing) and social multiplication (influencing authentic users to
    spread content organically).

-   **Projection**: The strategic positioning of narratives, actors, or
    operations to appear as originating from different sources,
    contexts, or motivations than their actual origins. This function
    enables operations to circumvent audience skepticism or regulatory
    constraints.

-   **Spillover**: The intentional or emergent spread of influence
    operations beyond their original target domains, audiences, or
    objectives. Spillover can create cascading effects that extend
    operational impact far beyond initial investment.

-   **Terrain Shaping**: Long-term efforts to modify the structural
    conditions within which information flows, including changes to
    platform algorithms, regulatory environments, social norms, or
    institutional practices that favor particular types of influence.

Function is analytically distinct from several related but separate
concepts:

-   **Visibility**: Whether actions are overt, covert, or operate in
    gray zones between transparency and secrecy. A function can be
    served through entirely transparent means (such as legitimate
    advocacy) or highly covert operations.

-   **Legality**: Whether actions comply with applicable laws and
    regulations. The same function might be served through legal
    lobbying in one jurisdiction and illegal coordination in another.

-   **Outcome**: The actual results achieved by influence operations,
    which may differ significantly from intended functions due to
    audience resistance, operational failures, or unintended
    consequences.

-   **Intent**: The stated or inferred motivations of actors, which may
    be deliberately misrepresented or may evolve as operations develop.

This framework provides a way to organize and understand diverse
manipulation behaviors based on their structural purpose within
influence operations. This enables analysts to identify functional
similarities across otherwise disparate activities and to predict how
different operational elements might interact or evolve.

## **Ethical Foundations and Scope** {#ethical-foundations-and-scope}

At its foundation, Infomap draws a crucial distinction between ethical
and unethical forms of influence, recognizing that influence itself is a
fundamental aspect of human communication and social organization. Not
all influence is malicious or problematic. Influence can be persuasive,
constructive, or protective, such as public health campaigns encouraging
vaccination, civic education promoting democratic participation, or
emergency communications directing people to evacuate during natural
disasters. These forms of influence typically operate through
transparent means, respect audience autonomy, and aim to serve broader
public interests.

Unethical influence differs fundamentally in several key dimensions.

-   First, it typically lacks appropriate disclosure about sources,
    funding, or true intentions, preventing audiences from making
    informed assessments about the information they receive.

-   Second, it demonstrates disrespect for human agency by seeking to
    manipulate rather than persuade, often exploiting cognitive biases,
    emotional vulnerabilities, or information asymmetries.

-   Third, it prioritizes narrow interests over broader social welfare,
    frequently externalizing costs onto communities, institutions, or
    democratic processes.

-   Finally, unethical influence operations often employ deception,
    misdirection, or coercion rather than relying on the strength of
    their arguments or evidence.

The distinction between ethical and unethical influence is not always
clear-cut, and context matters significantly. Persuasive techniques that
might be acceptable in commercial advertising, with appropriate
disclosure, could be problematic when applied to political discourse or
public health information. Similarly, the same information might serve
legitimate educational purposes in one context while functioning as part
of a manipulation campaign in another. Infomap's function-based approach
helps analysts navigate these ambiguities by focusing on systemic roles
rather than making categorical judgments about specific techniques or
content.

Disclosure and informed consent represent critical boundary conditions
for ethical influence. When audiences understand who is communicating
with them, why, and through what means, they can better evaluate the
information they receive and make autonomous decisions about how to
respond. Unethical influence operations systematically undermine these
conditions, creating information asymmetries that advantage manipulators
over their targets. Over time, this erosion of transparency and trust
degrades the foundation that enables free choice, informed discourse,
and mutual understanding—core prerequisites for functioning democratic
societies.

This degradation creates epistemic uncertainty—a phenomenon
characterized by increasing disagreement over basic facts, blurred lines
between opinion and factual reporting, declining trust in formerly
respected information sources, and reduced confidence in the possibility
of shared truth. The cumulative effect of sustained unethical influence
operations is not merely the success or failure of particular campaigns,
but the weakening of social capacity for collective reasoning and
decision-making.

## **Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference (FIMI)** {#foreign-information-manipulation-and-interference-(fimi)}

This framework pays particular attention to Foreign Information
Manipulation and Interference (FIMI)—a specific class of unethical
influence that involves state and state-adjacent actors operating across
national boundaries. FIMI refers to deliberate actions by foreign actors
aimed at distorting or manipulating the information environment of
target countries to influence perceptions, decisions, or behaviors in
ways that serve the foreign actor's strategic interests. Rather than
those of the target population.

FIMI operations may exploit diplomatic ties, media systems, educational
institutions, or societal vulnerabilities, often working through
unwitting intermediaries who genuinely believe they are pursuing
legitimate activities. This creates complex attribution challenges and
ethical dilemmas, as responding to FIMI risks harming innocent actors
who have been manipulated or co-opted without their knowledge. The
sophistication of modern FIMI operations lies not only in their
technical capabilities but in their ability to weaponize legitimate
institutions, democratic processes, and social dynamics against
themselves.

However, FIMI represents only one category within the broader landscape
of harmful, unethical influence. Domestic actors—including political
organizations, commercial entities, criminal networks, and extremist
groups—can employ similar functions and techniques to manipulate
information environments for their purposes. Corporate campaigns
designed to obscure public health risks, political operations that
systematically mislead voters about electoral processes, or organized
crime networks that undermine investigative journalism all represent
threats to information integrity. These may not involve foreign actors
but require analytical attention and response.

The international dimension of FIMI creates particular challenges
because it often exploits the asymmetry between open and closed
information environments. Authoritarian states can wage influence
operations against democratic societies while protecting their
information spaces from reciprocal influence, creating structural
advantages for actors willing to operate unethically. This asymmetry
extends beyond state-level dynamics to include any situation where
actors with greater resources, fewer ethical constraints, or less
accountability can exploit the openness and good faith assumptions of
more transparent systems.

## **Defending Information Openness** {#defending-information-openness}

This framework rests on a foundational premise: that open information
environments constitute a fundamental public good worthy of robust
protection, even as we acknowledge their inherent vulnerabilities. This
position reflects both democratic theory and empirical evidence
demonstrating the relationship between information freedom and human
flourishing across political, scientific, and cultural dimensions.

Open information environments serve multiple critical functions in
democratic societies. They enable the contestation of ideas necessary
for political legitimacy, provide the transparency required for
institutional accountability, and create conditions under which
marginalized voices can challenge dominant narratives. They support
scientific advancement through peer review, replication, and open
critique. They foster cultural evolution by protecting space for
artistic expression, dissent, and the exploration of alternative
perspectives.

Most fundamentally, open information environments enable both individual
and collective self-determination. When people have access to diverse
information sources and freedom to evaluate competing claims without
manipulation, they can make more autonomous decisions about their lives
and participate meaningfully in shaping their societies. This capacity
for informed choice represents not merely a procedural value but a
constitutive element of human dignity and democratic legitimacy.

**Critically, open information environments enable both individual and
collective self-determination.** When people are free from unethical
influence and have access to diverse sources of information and the
freedom to evaluate competing claims, they gain autonomy and participate
in shaping their societies. This capacity for informed choice represents
not just a procedural value but a constitutive element of human dignity
and democratic legitimacy.

This openness creates an asymmetry that authoritarian despots, criminal
networks, tyrannical elements of society, and other malicious actors
systematically exploit. Closed information systems can project influence
into open ones while protecting themselves from reciprocal influence or
scrutiny. Actors willing to operate without ethical constraints can
exploit the good-faith assumptions that make open discourse possible.
They can so agitate and frustrate an open information environment that
the public turns on the very notion of an open system.

Those with greater resources can overwhelm authentic voices through
coordinated inauthentic behavior and evidence-based exploitation that
functions as perception hacking.

This asymmetry poses a fundamental strategic challenge: how to preserve
the openness that makes information environments valuable while
developing resilience against their systematic exploitation. Traditional
responses have often fallen into two problematic categories. The first
involves content-based restrictions that risk reproducing the very
censorship that open societies seek to avoid. The second involves
platform-level interventions that may inadvertently privilege certain
voices or perspectives over others, potentially undermining the
pluralism that openness is meant to protect.

Both approaches risk what we might call the “authoritarian's dilemma in
reverse”—the possibility that defending democracy requires adopting
undemocratic methods that ultimately undermine the values they claim to
protect. The challenge is therefore not simply technical but
philosophical: developing defensive strategies that strengthen rather
than compromise the democratic character of open information
environments.

This requires moving beyond reactive measures toward more sophisticated
approaches that can distinguish between legitimate discourse and
manipulative operations without relying on subjective judgments about
content quality or political perspective. It demands strategies that
enhance rather than restrict the capacity for public reasoning, that
increase rather than decrease transparency about information sources and
flows, and that empower rather than infantilize audiences in their
interactions with complex information environments.

Such strategies must operate across multiple levels simultaneously. They
require technological innovations that can detect coordinated
inauthentic behavior without creating new forms of surveillance or
control. They demand institutional adaptations that can respond rapidly
to emerging threats while maintaining democratic accountability and rule
of law constraints. They necessitate educational approaches that build
public understanding of manipulation tactics without fostering
paralyzing skepticism about all information sources.

They require what might be called “offensive defense”—proactive efforts
to strengthen the positive functions that open information environments
serve rather than simply trying to eliminate their negative
exploitation. This includes supporting independent journalism, funding
media literacy education, designing platforms that reward authentic
engagement over viral manipulation, and creating economic incentives
that favor information quality over mere attention capture.

Infomap was conceived and developed to support this more sophisticated
and proactive approach. Rather than providing another tool for content
removal or source blocking, it offers a framework for understanding the
systemic functions that different forms of influence serve within
broader information architectures. This functional perspective enables
analysts and decision-makers to develop interventions that target
manipulative structures while preserving legitimate communicative
practices that may employ similar techniques or technologies.

The framework supports several complementary defensive strategies. It
enables early warning systems that can detect emerging influence
operations before they achieve significant impact, allowing for more
targeted and less disruptive responses. It facilitates coordination
among different defensive actors—from platform companies to government
agencies to civil society organizations—by providing a common analytical
vocabulary that transcends institutional boundaries and jurisdictional
limitations.

Perhaps most importantly, Infomap supports public education efforts that
can help audiences develop more sophisticated understandings of how
influence operations work. Rather than simply warning people to be
skeptical of information they encounter online, the framework enables
more nuanced explanations of how authentic and inauthentic influence
differ, how manipulative operations exploit particular vulnerabilities.
What kinds of behavioral changes might increase resilience without
sacrificing the benefits of open information engagement.

The ultimate goal is not to eliminate all forms of influence or
manipulation—an impossible and potentially counterproductive
objective—but to shift the balance of power within information
environments back toward transparency, informed choice, and democratic
deliberation. This requires sustained effort across multiple domains and
cannot be achieved through any single intervention or framework.
However, by providing better tools for understanding how influence
operations function systemically, Infomap aims to contribute to broader
efforts to preserve and strengthen the open information environments
upon which human flourishing increasingly depends.

## **Framework Applications and Integration** {#framework-applications-and-integration}

The Infomap framework is designed for strategic use across multiple
professional domains, each of which brings distinct perspectives and
capabilities to the challenge of understanding and responding to
influence operations.

-   Policy makers can use the framework to assess regulatory gaps,
    design more effective interventions, and coordinate responses across
    agencies and jurisdictions.

-   Intelligence professionals can employ it to improve collection
    priorities, analytical products, and warning systems.

-   Journalists can apply it to investigate influence networks, explain
    complex operations to public audiences, and protect their reporting
    from manipulation.

-   Researchers can use it to develop a better theoretical understanding
    of influence dynamics, test empirical hypotheses, and evaluate the
    effectiveness of different countermeasures.

-   Civil society organizations can leverage the framework to document
    threats to information integrity, advocate for stronger protections,
    and educate communities about manipulation tactics.

-   Technology companies can use it to improve their detection systems,
    design more resilient platforms, and better understand the systemic
    implications of their policies.

-   International organizations can employ it to coordinate cross-border
    responses, share threat intelligence, and develop normative
    frameworks for addressing FIMI and related challenges.

The modular nature of Infomap enables integration with existing
analytical frameworks and technical systems. Its function-based approach
complements rather than replaces other analytical lenses, such as the
DISARM Framework's focus on specific tactics, techniques, and
procedures, or MISP's emphasis on technical indicators and threat
intelligence sharing. By focusing on systemic functions rather than
specific implementations, Infomap can help analysts identify connections
and patterns across different operational contexts while maintaining
compatibility with established workflows and tools.

## **Restoring Agency in Information Environments** {#restoring-agency-in-information-environments}

At its heart, Infomap is about restoring visibility, accountability, and
agency in the face of hidden influence. The framework recognizes that
perfect detection or elimination of unethical influence operations is
neither achievable nor necessarily desirable, as overly aggressive
responses risk creating new forms of censorship or control. The
framework, therefore, serves not as an endpoint but as a foundation for
ongoing work to protect and strengthen open information environments in
an era of sophisticated influence operations.

What follows is a detailed guide to the Infomap framework, introducing
each influence function, explaining their interactions and
interdependencies, and mapping how influence flows across different
domains and scales of operation. The framework is presented as a
practical tool for analysis and response, but also as a contribution to
broader efforts to understand how information, power, and democracy
interact in an increasingly complex and contested global environment.
