Projection Abroad — Chapter

Projection Abroad encompasses instruments used to extend domestic influence capacity into foreign environments. These include diaspora mobilization, state-aligned aid, and cyber units like Fancy Bear and Killnet. Cultural and religious networks are utilized to establish soft power and influence foreign public perceptions. Nuclear and infrastructure deals become levers of control through elite co-optation. Religious manipulation reframes moral hierarchies to undercut adversaries. All projection efforts aim to reproduce control, legitimacy, and narrative dominance beyond national borders.

Overview

Projection Abroad encompasses the deployment of influence mechanisms outside a state’s borders to shape foreign environments in ways that advance domestic strategic priorities. This is not about conventional soft power. It is about strategic extrusion: extending control, legitimacy, and narrative dominance into foreign systems using a range of instruments, from covert cyber units to cultural diplomacy and religious manipulation.

Unlike Operational Multipliers, which scale influence within an ecosystem, or Legitimation, which manufactures credibility around a message, Projection Abroad places assets inside foreign societies. These mechanisms manipulate host-country institutions, identities, and public opinion by embedding strategic narratives and capabilities in organic or independent entities.

Projection is not passive outreach—it is embedded activation. These tactics simulate local resonance while serving foreign state agendas through diaspora mobilization, targeted foreign aid, cyber campaigns, or cultural and religious proxies.

Varieties

Abuse of Diaspora

Diaspora communities are not instruments of state control. They are transnational populations shaped by displacement, war, labor migration, or exile, carrying memories, identities, and frequently trauma. Diasporas are not inherently political actors, nor should they be treated as proxies for regimes they fled.

Yet authoritarian states regularly exploit diaspora communities, capitalizing on language, kinship ties, cultural memories, and trust networks to extend coercive power across borders (Tsourapas 2020; Adamson 2019). Just as these regimes abuse open societies’ freedoms—free speech protections and democratic tolerance—to launder manipulated information or incite division, they exploit diaspora communities as cover for influence operations. This exploitation reflects not on the diaspora itself, but rather on how hostile states weaponize the social cohesion and freedoms diasporas embody.

The scale of the problem is now well documented. Freedom House research shows that between 2014 and 2024, 48 governments committed acts of physical transnational repression against individuals living in 103 host countries, with the number of perpetrator states, host countries, and incidents growing every year (Schenkkan 2025; Vaughan, Gorokhovskaia, and Schenkkan 2025). China is the most prolific perpetrator, but Russia, Turkey, Iran, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and others are active practitioners (Vaughan, Gorokhovskaia, and Schenkkan 2025; Human Rights Watch 2024). The tactics range from assassination and rendition to digital surveillance, harassment of family members at home, and coercion-by-proxy through community organizations and overseas police service stations (Safeguard Defenders 2022; Wong 2024; Moss 2016).

These incursions are part of a broader authoritarian strategy that exploits the openness of liberal societies, including freedom of expression, religious liberty, academic independence, and multicultural pluralism. The issue is not freedom itself, but the asymmetric exploitation of that freedom by regimes that deny such liberties at home.

The consequences extend well beyond individual targets. Transnational repression spreads fear, mistrust, and division throughout entire diaspora communities, leading individuals to self-censor, withdraw from political participation, or isolate from their own communities (Moss 2016; Michaelsen and Anstis 2025). Women human rights defenders and journalists in exile face particular, gendered forms of digital threats that exploit their gender identity to intimidate, shame, and discredit them (Michaelsen and Anstis 2025). Second-generation diaspora members—whose skills, resources, and multi-sited embeddedness make them especially valuable to authoritarian regimes—face targeted engagement or repression strategies designed to harness or neutralize their potential (Wackenhut and Orjuela 2023). In China’s case, the state leverages nationalist sentiments to alienate dissidents and cultivate loyalist diaspora networks that conduct surveillance, harassment, and assault against Hong Kongers, Tibetans, Uyghurs, Taiwanese, and mainland Chinese critics abroad (Wong 2024; Oztig and Karluk 2024).

This issue is not merely about diaspora manipulation. It is fundamentally about democratic failure.

When authoritarian regimes operate within democratic societies—coercing individuals, hijacking community institutions, exporting surveillance, and manipulating narratives—they are not engaging in mere meddling. They are directly violating sovereignty. Such intrusions are not subtle or symbolic; they are blatant acts of aggression carried out with alarming impunity.

The responsibility for these violations lies not with the diaspora communities but with the governments that fail to protect them. Democratic states have repeatedly allowed adversarial powers to conduct hostile operations on their territory, targeting their own residents without meaningful consequences, exposure, or deterrence. They have blurred critical distinctions between cultural exchange and covert infiltration, abandoning exiles who fled authoritarian regimes only to face their oppressors’ reach unimpeded abroad. Indeed, restrictive asylum policies in many democracies compound the vulnerability of potential targets, who—without permanent legal status—remain dependent on their home countries for documents and are thus exposed to continued pressure (Freedom House 2025).

This failure creates predictable consequences. Without enforcement, intrusion becomes normalized, and without consequence, boundaries erode. When democratic governments hesitate, adversaries escalate their aggression. Six states enacted their first documented instance of physical transnational repression in 2023 alone (Vaughan, Gorokhovskaia, and Schenkkan 2025). The problem has not peaked: the consolidation of modernized, globally integrated authoritarian states, the subversion of international norms against extraterritorial violence, and the pace of technological change all make continued growth likely (Schenkkan 2025).

This is not an isolated incident—it is a pervasive pattern occurring openly in capital cities, university campuses, cultural institutions, and digital spaces throughout the democratic world. The message sent to aggressors and allies alike is clear and deeply damaging: democratic sovereignty can be negotiated, harassment and coercion are acceptable if carried out discreetly, and citizen and resident rights are conditionally protected.

This must end.

Democracies must cease mistaking restraint for strength. They must accurately label these incursions as hostile operations and meet them decisively with a clear, coordinated, and uncompromising response.

Sovereignty is not symbolic; it is a state’s fundamental obligation. Failing to defend sovereignty—in the name of caution, optics, or inertia—does not enhance democratic principles; it betrays them.

Cultural Infrastructure

State actors establish or finance permanent cultural institutions, educational centers, and exchange networks abroad to entrench influence within foreign societies. Unlike short-term cultural initiatives, these structures operate as durable nodes of projection, designed to sustain ideological, linguistic, and relational continuity across generations.

Operating under the banner of cultural diplomacy, they embed regime personnel, narratives, and funding within host-country institutions, shaping perception from inside trusted civic and academic spaces. Through language instruction, research partnerships, cultural centers, and “friendship societies,” these entities function as soft-power outposts that both disseminate national ideology and cultivate networks of sympathetic elites.

Soviet friendship societies, for example, targeted foreign youth, artists, and writers, drawing them into curated experiences that celebrated Marxist values (CIA 1955; Huang and Xiang 2019). Contemporary analogs include Russian cultural centers and Confucius Institutes, which China supports, as well as programs that curate images of peaceful, traditionalist, or anti-imperialist regimes while avoiding critical scrutiny (ruskidom.rs 2023; Hybrid Warfare Analytical Group 2021; U.S. Government Accountability Office 2019; GAO 2023).

The core objective is presence projection—to reproduce domestic systems of narrative control abroad, ensuring that cultural familiarity evolves into political alignment.

  • Post-Soviet adaptations blend cultural outreach with academic collaboration. Entities such as the Russian International Affairs Council and the Russkiy Mir Foundation combine genuine scholarship with narrative engineering (Hybrid Warfare Analytical Group 2021; Kožnárek and Stojarová 2025; Jeangène Vilmer et al. 2018; Kamyshanskaya 2021; Redden 2013).

  • Russkiy Mir promotes the Russian language and culture abroad while sponsoring conferences and publications that advance the “Russian World” ideology—a civilizational framework linking culture to territorial destiny (Hybrid Warfare Analytical Group 2021; Kožnárek and Stojarová 2025; Milosevich 2020a).

  • GAO reports on Confucius Institutes document contractual provisions granting Beijing control over programming and personnel, embedding propaganda under the pretense of language education (GAO 2023; U.S. Government Accountability Office 2019; Huang and Xiang 2019).

A more recent example:

  • Leaked documents show that Aleksandr Chepurnoy, director of the Russian cultural center in Alicante, coordinated anti-NATO actions and Moscow-aligned protests in Spain, even after EU sanctions targeted his state-linked foundation, Pravfond. Chepurnoy organized pressure campaigns—including blocking a photo exhibit on Russian war crimes—and cultivated local allies to advance Kremlin interests. Despite sanctions, Pravfond continued funding influence operations across Europe, often working with intelligence operatives, and has faced similar scrutiny in Australia and elsewhere.

Malignant Foreign Aid

Foreign assistance is typically framed as development, relief, or capacity-building. Malignant foreign aid subverts this model by utilizing economic support as a means of political dependency and a strategic compliance lever.

By funding specific infrastructure, security sectors, or political movements, regimes create embedded obligations in recipient governments. This aid comes with implicit expectations: silence on human rights, support in multilateral fora, or alignment in media narratives.

The power of this mechanism lies in its duality: it appears benevolent but functions coercively. It fosters asymmetrical relationships masked by humanitarian language.

Oligarchic Mercenary Companies

Oligarchic mercenary companies such as the Wagner Group represent a sophisticated evolution of state violence: a fusion of military coercion, commercial exploitation, and narrative warfare—all under the veil of deniability. These organizations function not as rogue actors but as extralegal arms of the regime, used to project power, maintain plausible deniability, and entrench influence across global theaters.

Though labeled a “private military company” (PMC), Wagner has never operated independently (Gostev and Coalson 2016; Fontanka 2016; Jones 2020). Ex-GRU commander Dmitry Utkin founded the organization, which was effectively run by the Russian oligarch Yevgeny Prigozhin, whose tight relationships with Russian intelligence and defense leaders have been extensively documented (OCCRP 2025).

Investigations have revealed that Wagner operatives were trained at GRU-adjacent facilities in Molkyno and that Prigozhin maintained regular contact with senior figures, including Defense Minister Sergei Shoigu and GRU General Alexey Dyumin. Wagner’s command structure thus lies within the orbit of Russian military intelligence, and its operational freedom depends on state protection (Wikipedia contributors 2025a; Reuters 2024).

Despite its nominal illegality—private military companies remain neither fully legal nor banned under Russian law—Wagner was heavily deployed by the Russian state in Crimea, Donbas, Syria, Libya, Sudan, the Central African Republic (CAR), and Mali (Wikipedia contributors 2024, 2025b; Ber 2023; Sherlock 2025; Elbagir et al. 2023; Lebedev, Light, and Donati 2024; Human Rights Watch 2022).

Wagner performed state-like functions in many regions: training foreign forces, managing VIP protection, conducting election influence campaigns, extracting resources (notably gold, oil, and rare minerals), and targeting journalists. In CAR alone, Wagner operatives have been implicated in murders of investigative reporters, UN-targeted attacks, and systematic extrajudicial executions (Mudge 2020; Doxsee and Thompson 2022).

The company also oversaw front entities, such as AFRIC (Association for Free Research and International Cooperation) and Africa Back Office, which were tasked with developing and deploying influence strategies—combining military intimidation with politically driven propaganda, economic infiltration, and ethnographic manipulation (U.S. Department of the Treasury 2021; US Department of State 2022; OpenSanctions 2023). These units coordinated directly with Russia’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of Defense, collapsing the public-private divide.

Oligarchic mercenary companies like Wagner operate in both the physical and cognitive planes while helping a regime specifically avoid things like diplomatic recognition:

  • Physically, they act as an irregular army, capable of force projection, regime protection, and deterrence.

  • Cognitively, they instill fear, signal regime reach, suppress journalism, and provide “muscle” for hybrid influence campaigns.

Their utility lies in strategic ambiguity. Wagner can be activated globally without triggering formal war thresholds or diplomatic consequences. When caught, it is disavowed. When successful, it reshapes realities in favor of Russian interests—militarily, economically, and ideologically.

Even after the failed 2023 mutiny and the apparent death of Prigozhin, Wagner’s model has persisted. Reports suggest that its activities are fragmented into successor companies, such as RPOST-M LLC in Syria, which continue operations under new names while preserving structural continuity (مراد 2024). This modular restructuring allows the Kremlin to preserve the strategic value of coercive proxy forces while diffusing liability and reasserting control.

In this way, oligarchic mercenary companies are not merely battlefield tools. They are command and control extensions of the regime itself, carrying out geopolitically sensitive missions that no formal institution could claim—and no adversary can easily counter.

Spiritual Exploitation

Spiritual exploitation refers to the deliberate appropriation and instrumentalization of religious symbols, language, institutions, and identities for strategic political or ideological objectives. Within the framework of Soviet and post-Soviet information operations, this tactic functions as a powerful tool to mobilize emotional resonance, construct legitimacy, and neutralize dissent.

At the core of spiritual exploitation lies the conversion of metaphysical authority into political capital. Religious symbols—such as crosses, cathedrals, saints, and rituals—are repurposed to imbue political narratives with transcendent significance. The state positions itself as the guardian of “traditional values” or a divinely sanctioned moral order. In doing so, it cloaks strategic interests in sacred rhetoric, making policy appear preordained and morally unassailable.

This mechanism often involves the blurring of secular and spiritual vocabularies. Terms like “decadence,” “redemption,” and “civilizational mission” enter geopolitical discourse, allowing ideological goals to be framed not as contingent preferences but as existential imperatives (Elkner 2005).

Spiritual exploitation enables regimes to manufacture a moral high ground from which to launch ideological offensives. This is particularly salient in diasporic or post-colonial environments, where religious identity may serve as a key marker of belonging and resistance.

For example, Russia’s post-2000 turn toward “Orthodox nationalism” uses the Russian Orthodox Church to portray the Kremlin as the rightful heir to a besieged civilizational heritage (Bratersky 2012; European Union 2022; Payne 2010; European Values 2012). This narrative casts the West as spiritually degenerate and morally corrupt, thereby justifying both domestic authoritarianism and foreign intervention as acts of civilizational defense (Stoeckl 2020; Milosevich 2020a, 2020b; Elkner 2005).

By co-opting religious identity, the state extends its influence over ethno-religious diasporas. The concept of the Russkiy Mir (“Russian World”) exemplifies this, weaving together language, culture, and Orthodox Christianity into a framework of soft power that spans borders (Hybrid Warfare Analytical Group 2021; Milosevich 2020a). The effect is a form of “soft annexation,” where cultural and religious affinity becomes a pathway to political allegiance (Shikerova and Wesolowsky 2022; Milosevich 2020a; European Values 2012).

In these contexts, spiritual discourse is not merely persuasive—it is immunizing. By operating in the register of the sacred, it renders counter-narratives suspect or sacrilegious. Dissent is not just disagreement; it is heresy.

This exploitation is particularly effective when aligned with genuine structures of belief. When state narratives align with sincerely held spiritual values, they elicit genuine affirmation rather than resistance. Such operations operate beneath the threshold of critical scrutiny, engaging audiences at the level of identity and existential security.

The use of spiritual vocabulary also provides aesthetic and emotional depth to propaganda. Visuals of candlelit processions, clerical blessings of weapons, and references to divine missions or martyrdom engage the limbic system, circumventing rational critique.

Spiritual exploitation is not just symbolic; it is strategically rationalized. As Soviet active measures manuals and GRU psychological warfare training have indicated, emotional and symbolic mobilization are central to effective influence operations. In hybrid warfare, the fusion of spiritual, cultural, and informational vectors can erode the epistemic boundaries between religion and politics, truth and myth, thereby generating cognitive ambiguity in target populations.

State-Sanctioned Cyber Activity

Cyber operations executed by state-directed units or regime-aligned “hacktivist” groups represent the digital vanguard of projection. These campaigns target foreign governments, journalists, companies, and civil infrastructure to exfiltrate data, disrupt operations, or leak curated material that serves the regime’s strategic narrative.

Notable units, such as Fancy Bear (APT28), conduct persistent intrusions, intelligence gathering, and targeted leaks designed to undermine trust, foment division, or damage political adversaries (CrowdStrike 2019; Roussi 2022). Groups like Killnet, while presenting as grassroots or criminally independent, execute coordinated disruptive campaigns (DDoS attacks) in alignment with state interests.

These actors operate in the liminal space between espionage and narrative warfare—covert in operation, but overt in impact. Their goal is not merely to collect secrets but to reshape public perception through digital coercion.

A Platform for Asymmetric Leverage

Projection mechanisms do not simply send messages—they install capabilities. They recreate the operational logic of domestic control in foreign environments, utilizing familiar institutions—such as aid, religion, civil society, and cyber infrastructure—as platforms for asymmetric leverage.

They are often challenging to identify because they co-opt legitimate forms. Cultural exchange becomes cultural conditioning. Humanitarian aid becomes a political tethering. Religious affinity becomes ideological fusion. Electoral engagement becomes information laundering.

Projection abroad is not an invasion. It is integration with intent—the quiet reproduction of regime influence beneath the skin of another society.

In the chapters ahead, we examine more disruptive functions: Sabotage, which targets cohesion, and Obfuscation, which hides the hand. Projection is neither, but it sets the conditions for both.
