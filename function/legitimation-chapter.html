FIMI Taxonomy v4.1 — Modular taxonomy of foreign information manipulation and interference

Legitimation — Chapter

chapter
Author
Affiliation

E. Rosalie Li

Information Epidemiology Lab

Published

February 24, 2026

Legitimation is the strategic function through which regimes manufacture the appearance of authenticity, consensus, and moral credibility for their narratives and actions. Unlike brute force or overt censorship, legitimation cloaks manipulation in the garments of trust—academia, civil society, science, culture, and grassroots activism. It transforms propaganda from an act of coercion into one of persuasion, making ideology seem organic rather than imposed. In essence, legitimation “launders” intent: embedding regime interests within familiar and seemingly independent institutions so that hostile narratives gain a sheen of credibility. This stands in contrast to the Command and Control function of influence (see the Command and Control chapter), which is about synchronization and dominance; legitimation, instead, is about credibility and consent. It leverages cultural soft power, epistemic authority, and moral language to stabilize influence efforts, create echo chambers of support, and undermine critical resistance. By weaponizing reputable platforms and voices, legitimation makes state-sponsored narratives appear normal, popular, or expert-endorsed rather than driven by a regime.

Many of these methods were first institutionalized by the Soviet Union through KGB Active Measures (Directorate A) and the International Department of the Communist Party (Andrew & Mitrokhin, 2000; Rid, 2020). They have not vanished—they have migrated, adapted, and reappeared across modern authoritarian regimes under new guises, from “multipolarity” to “sovereign democracy.” Today’s authoritarian states build on that legacy with updated technologies and global reach, but the underlying goal remains the same: launder official narratives through seemingly independent channels. This creates an ecosystem of front organizations, sympathetic voices, and pseudo-independent initiatives that together sustain a disinformation architecture. Legitimation functions as the backbone of this architecture, continually supplying each campaign with signs and symbols of credibility and legitimacy.

Legitimation is an operational layer of influence, not merely a rhetorical style. It provides the credibility infrastructure that other influence functions rely on. A regime can flood social media with fake accounts (Obfuscation) or command proxies to amplify messages (Operational Multipliers), but without some veneer of legitimacy, these messages won’t stick. Legitimation efforts inject that veneer: planting stories in respected journals, having sympathetic “experts” echo talking points on news panels, or sponsoring cultural events that align the regime with shared humanistic values. In so doing, legitimation blurs the line between genuine civic discourse and state-driven messaging. As later sections will show, this involves a wide range of tactics—from state-backed activist fronts to scientific mimicry—all converging on the same objective: make the state’s preferred position look and feel credible.

Legitimation operates by mimicry and mirroring. Authoritarian influence agents often mimic the forms of legitimate democratic discourse (protests, petitions, scholarly research, philanthropic grants, awards ceremonies) while inverting their function. A youth festival or peace conference in an open society is a forum for genuine cultural exchange; a similar event funded by an authoritarian state is engineered to echo that state’s worldview. By exploiting the openness of democratic systems—free press, academic freedom, pluralism of civil society—authoritarian actors effectively weaponize legitimate institutions against themselves. This creates complex attribution challenges, as many unwitting intermediaries (journalists, scholars, activists) may genuinely believe they are pursuing independent activities, not realizing they have been co-opted. As a result, responses to legitimation tactics require great care: blunt countermeasures risk harming innocent actors or undermining the very openness that disinformation campaigns exploit.

This section explores the varieties of legitimation tactics and examines how each one works. For each tactic, a historical or contemporary example has been included. The subsections highlight connections to other influence functions. Through these examples, the work of legitimation can be considered an ecosystem of credibility that sustains information manipulation. This ecosystem is modular and resilient: if one “asset”—say, a fake NGO or a covert influencer—is exposed, another can replace it, and the illusion of independent support often endures. Legitimation is thus not a peripheral embellishment to influence operations; it is central to their endurance and impact.

Varieties

State-Backed Activism

State-backed activism simulates grassroots mobilization to legitimize regime interests. Its purpose is to create the illusion of broad popular support for authoritarian policies or opposition to their adversaries. Through front groups, proxy NGOs, or inserted participants, regimes stage protests, peace movements, and digital campaigns that appear to arise from civil society while actually serving state direction. By obscuring their sponsorship, these movements transform coercion into what appears to be spontaneous civic will.

The Soviet-era World Peace Council exemplified this practice, organizing anti-NATO demonstrations while receiving KGB funding and strategic guidance (Alexiev, 1985; the1971?). Modern versions include state-backed “anti-fascist” or “anti-colonial” collectives that frame authoritarian regimes as defenders of justice and sovereignty (Whitman, 2023; Young, 2024; page2025?). The tactic’s strength lies in its plausibility: movements appear organic, journalists mistake them for genuine activism, and observers misread state propaganda as citizen sentiment.

  • A high-profile Soviet front group composed of 13 former NATO generals who advocated nuclear disarmament and the removal of U.S. troops from Europe. Though not overtly directed by the KGB, it was created and funded by the World Peace Council (WPC), a known Soviet front (the1971?)

  • Presenting themselves as a patriotic biker group, the Night Wolves received over a million dollars in donations over several years, starting in 2014 (Navalny, 2015; Shikerova & Wesolowsky, 2022). Their goal was to promote secessionist movements and anti-Western sentiments in Europe.

Contemporary evidence of such malign influence continues to surface(Baker, 2025). Responding effectively requires precision: participants may act sincerely, unaware of manipulation. Treating them as conscious collaborators risks validating the adversary’s framing of repression. Yet inaction allows the deception to persist. A calibrated approach—exposing coordination while distinguishing genuine activism from instrumentalization—denies authoritarian regimes both martyrdom and invisibility.

Education remains the most potent countermeasure. Once sincere movements realize that foreign backing almost always destroys credibility, they self-isolate from manipulation. Coalitions fracture, funding evaporates, and reputations collapse when covert sponsorship becomes public. History shows that foreign alignment seldom advances activist goals—it corrodes them.

Awards and Philanthropy

Authoritarian regimes use awards, grants, and philanthropic gestures to recruit sympathizers and validate aligned voices (Bader, 2025; Huang & Xiang, 2019; Kiseleva, 2015; Lien & Miao, 2023; Mäkinen, 2016; china2024?). By bestowing honors upon journalists, academics, clerics, or politicians, they create an aura of moral or intellectual legitimacy that strengthens narrative alignment.

These honors serve three overlapping functions: elevating aligned figures by rewarding conformity with prestige, laundering regime narratives through seemingly independent institutions, and cultivating dependency by offering access, recognition, or material benefit.

  • The Serena Shim Award, for instance, was presented to former employees of Russian state media outlets to portray them as courageous truth-tellers (Whitaker, 2021)

  • In 2016, the Kremlin financed the “Dialogue of Nations” conference, convening global secessionist movements—from Texas to Catalonia—to foster anti-Western solidarity under the banner of self-determination (Mirovalev, 2016; Reigstad, 2016)

Awards function as a moral laundering mechanism: they convert propaganda into philanthropy. The recipients become willing conduits, repeating state-approved narratives as the price of continued recognition.

Intellectual Capture

Intellectual capture refers to the selective co-optation of foreign academics, think tank experts, journalists, or intellectual gatekeepers through incentives such as access, visibility, or institutional collaboration. Unlike overt propaganda or espionage, this is not about recruiting operatives but cultivating sympathizers or leveraging existing contrarians.

Through speaking invitations, co-authored research, participation in exclusive roundtables, travel, public praise, or privileged access to archives and policymakers, regimes can shape how their narratives are framed in foreign discourse without dictating the message.

Unlike malign academic initiatives, which originate within adversarial regimes and are designed to appear scholarly while promoting propaganda, intellectual capture is a flexible, opportunistic strategy. It occurs on a case-by-case basis, targeting individuals who already straddle the boundary between criticizing democratic institutions and being receptive to counter-narratives.

The regime need not overtly direct their work; normalization is enough. If a foreign academic describes Russia’s invasion of Ukraine as a “security response to NATO encroachment” or attributes public protests in Belarus solely to CIA meddling, the effect is achieved. The regime’s message reaches mainstream discussions through a voice that prompts people to consider it more seriously. Critically, many of these claims work because they are rooted in past errors (Little, 2022; Snow & Snow, 2020). It speaks to the gravity of decisions we make today.

What makes intellectual capture effective is the perceived, and sometimes real, independence. Scholars do not need to endorse authoritarian policies explicitly; simply reframing a debate in terms favorable to the regime (“Russophobia,” “civilizational struggle,” “Western double standards”) can distort public understanding (Stradner, 2022; THE_WASHINGTON_POST2024?). These frameworks are subtle shifts in emphasis, terminology, and interpretation. They become much more resilient once they appear in otherwise reputable journals, panels, or think tanks, as these become the sources cited by journalists and reporters.

Access is both the carrot and the filter. Regimes grant selected scholars access to officials, databases, or leaks that cannot be verified elsewhere, effectively gating strategic insight behind narrative compliance. Participation in groups, such as the Valdai Discussion Club or the Eurasian Economic Forum, becomes a reward structure for ideological accommodation (Cia, 1955; Kamyshanskaya, 2021; Redden, 2013; ref2024?). Those who deviate are quietly excluded, denied visas, or de-platformed in partner institutions.

The outcome is not silencing dissent but the soft sanitization of critique. In this ecosystem, to point out that a study’s conclusions echo a regime’s propaganda line becomes “Russophobia.” Questioning the legitimacy of data from an authoritarian government-linked institute is perceived as “biased.” Accountability itself is reframed as intolerance or hypocrisy. The line between analysis and alignment dissolves, replaced by a fog of intellectual ambiguity in which authoritarian narratives overtake reality-based content under the guise of concern for balance and fairness.

Malign Academic Initiatives

Malign academic initiatives are state-funded institutions that simulate scholarship while producing ideologically predetermined conclusions. Unlike intellectual capture, which co-opts external experts, these initiatives originate within the regime itself. Their function is to convert propaganda into research—to clothe ideology in academic form.

Soviet precedent is instructive. The Institute for the Study of the USA and Canada produced Cold War “research” framing racial inequality and capitalism as fatal flaws of liberal democracy (McNeill et al., 1982). Its modern analogues—spanning Russia, China, and Iran—issue white papers, host conferences, and produce data designed to delegitimize the liberal order. They mimic the aesthetics of academia—ISSNs, editorial boards, peer-review façades—but begin with answers, not questions (Cia, 1955).

  • In Russia, state-affiliated think tanks within the Russian Academy of Sciences publish reports that recast repression as national security and democracy assistance as subversion . Their authors hold formal doctorates and security credentials, giving political messaging an aura of analytic depth (CIA, 1975)

  • China’s think tanks, such as the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences (CASS), propagate slogans like “democracy that works” and “Community of Common Destiny for Mankind,” framing one-party rule as modern governance (Chen, 2021; Sleeboom-Faulkner, 2007). Iran’s Islamic Propaganda Organization employs similar techniques, producing “academic” reports on Western moral decline that later reappear in state-aligned media as independent research.

Both Russia’s warfare concept and China’s “discourse power” theory identify pseudo-academic justification as a core element of information warfare. The KGB’s Operation Denver, which fabricated scientific “evidence” that the Pentagon created HIV/AIDS, was a prototype (Kramer, 2020; Selvage, 2021; selvage2019?). The same pattern persisted in efforts to dispute chemical attacks in Syria (Whitaker, 2021; team2018?).

Once published, such “research” enters a feedback loop. State media cite it as third-party validation; officials quote the media; international commentators then repeat both, constructing a synthetic consensus. Authors are rewarded with promotion, visibility, and protection (Kovalik et al., 2024; RTUKnews, 2017; vanessabeeley, 2017; VanessaBeeley, 2018). When reprinted abroad—sometimes stripped of attribution—their falsehoods circulate as legitimate references (Whitaker, 2021; alex2020?; team2018?; douma2018?).

This is not research—it is the weaponization of its appearance. By simulating epistemic authority, malign academic initiatives turn credibility into a strategic asset.

Cultural Initiatives

Regimes organize, fund, or co-opt artistic, educational, and cultural programs to project an image of sophistication, openness, and shared human values while embedding ideological cues within seemingly apolitical forms (Cia, 1955; F., 1954; china2024?). These initiatives—festivals, exhibitions, exchanges, and performances—operate as symbolic acts of persuasion, designed to make authoritarian systems appear cultured and humane rather than coercive.

Their function is credibility laundering through culture: they borrow the moral authority of art, scholarship, and heritage to confer legitimacy on political power. The aim is affective, not infrastructural—to influence perception rather than to establish permanent presence. Once the event ends, the channel of influence dissolves, but the impression of refinement and normalcy remains.

Unlike cultural infrastructure, which embeds personnel and funding abroad to sustain long-term influence, cultural initiatives are ephemeral and performative—short-duration displays that normalize ideology through admiration rather than alignment. Over time, these recurring gestures soften international resistance, attract sympathetic elites, and transform propaganda into prestige, providing regimes with a renewable source of moral and aesthetic legitimacy.

Petitions and Polling

State and state-linked actors exploit petition and polling systems in open societies to insert authoritarian narratives into legitimate civic processes. By mimicking participatory rituals—crowdsourced petitions, citizen consultations, and public opinion surveys—they translate foreign influence into the language of democracy, disguising strategic messaging as expressions of local sentiment.

Petitions serve as the performative arm of this tactic. Regime-aligned networks organize or amplify campaigns that appear grassroots but are designed to simulate popular opposition or moral consensus around issues that advance state interests. In 2022–2023, Russian and pro-Russian groups promoted petitions such as Stop Arming Ukraine and No to NATO Expansion across Europe and the United States. Circulated through alternative media and sympathetic politicians, these campaigns were later cited by Russian state outlets as proof of Western “war fatigue” and “public opposition to escalation.” Similar operations surfaced in Slovakia, Hungary, and Bulgaria, often through nationalist parties with established Kremlin ties, creating the illusion of domestic resistance to NATO and EU policy.

Polling functions as the quantitative counterpart—a data-driven façade of legitimacy. Regime-linked firms or sympathetic consultants commission surveys abroad to generate “evidence” that Western publics support neutrality, oppose sanctions, or distrust allied institutions. These results are then amplified by state media as authentic indicators of global sentiment, closing the feedback loop between engineered data and manufactured legitimacy.

China employs comparable methods through diaspora and front organizations coordinated by the United Front Work Department, which organize petitions and open letters opposing legislation on Uyghur forced labor, TikTok restrictions, and Taiwan relations. Circulated among student associations and business groups abroad, these initiatives are later amplified by Chinese state outlets as proof that “overseas Chinese communities reject Western anti-China hysteria.”

As Grauvogel and von Soest note, such practices exemplify legitimation through emulation—the use of democratic procedures to validate authoritarian claims of popular support (Grauvogel & Soest, 2024). Once petitions or polls enter legislative records, media coverage, or policy debates, they acquire procedural legitimacy. State media then cite them as “evidence” of democratic endorsement, creating a closed feedback system of influence disguised as participation.

As Zaret observed in his study of early modern petitioning, the petition once expanded democracy by converting private grievance into public deliberation (Zaret, 1996). In the contemporary information battlespace, the same form has been repurposed as an instrument of projection—a symbolic import from democracy used to launder authoritarian narratives and legitimize their global reach.

Scientific Mimicry

Scientific mimicry involves producing or manipulating materials that appear scientific to support regime interests. Forced studies, distorted data, and pseudoscientific reports are presented in academic formats to disguise propaganda as evidence-based analysis. This tactic leverages public trust in scientific authority while undermining the integrity of epistemic institutions.

Soviet examples include fabricated studies linking the U.S. to biological warfare programs. More recently, Russian and Chinese outlets have pushed false studies about Western biolabs, vaccines, efficacy, or COVID-19 origins to sow distrust and shift blame. Scientific mimicry turns epistemic legitimacy into a weapon.

Unqualified Experts

Unqualified experts are individuals who are presented as authorities despite lacking relevant credentials or institutional affiliations. These figures act as surrogate analysts or commentators, echoing regime positions on international platforms while portraying themselves as independent voices.

Their appeal often lies in their outsider status—they appear to speak truth to power, exploiting contrarian credibility. During the Soviet era, such individuals were usually referred to as “peace researchers” or “military specialists.” Today, similar figures appear in fringe outlets or on social media, claiming geopolitical insight while subtly promoting regime narratives.

Publication Partnerships

Publication partnerships frequently operate as paid inserts, content‑sharing deals, and white‑label pipelines that move state messaging into mainstream venues. ⁠ Beyond traditional inserts, content laundering pathways let pieces originating on RT.com be mirrored or lightly reworked across hundreds of clone, aggregator, and pseudo‑local sites before resurfacing in discourse as “neutral” analysis (Schafer et al., 2024).

  • Afrique Média TV signed cooperation accords with the Kremlin-linked groups AFRIC and COSI, both of which work with RT Africa; reporting by the Atlantic Council and others confirms the collaboration’s impact on Ukraine-war coverage and Moscow’s messaging in Africa (Ratiu, 2024)
  • Global Research is a Canadian website intricately linked to Russia’s disinformation and propaganda efforts, featuring a wide array of fringe authors and conspiracy theorists. This platform has collaborated with Russian and Chinese websites since the early 2010s, providing a Western perspective that benefits the disinformation ecosystem (GLOBAL_ENGAGEMENT_CENTER2020?)
  • ISW News, a pro-Iran outlet, publicly listed SouthFront as a partner on its archived homepage.

⁠⁠The appearance of cooperation conceals asymmetric influence: one side seeks legitimacy, the other provides it. Over time, repetition of regime-aligned narratives in otherwise credible venues erodes the line between propaganda and expertise, and later corrections are easier to frame as politicized. Studies of intermedia agenda‑setting and quotation propagation show how state‑controlled outlets can nudge coverage patterns across the wider information ecosystem, subtly shifting salience and sentiment in downstream reporting.⁠

Artificial Volume

Artificial volume is the practice of flooding media ecosystems with repetitive or near-identical content across multiple outlets, creating the illusion of widespread consensus or high engagement. This saturation technique is particularly effective in fragile media environments, where repetition substitutes for credibility.

Through centralized coordination, authoritarian regimes publish cloned articles, synchronized commentaries, and mass social media posts reinforcing a message. The result is a distorted perception of reality in which targeted ideas appear dominant, urgent, or popular—even if entirely manufactured.

Circular Citations

Circular citation is a legitimizing loop in which regime-aligned claims are recycled across a network of publications, analysts, or NGOs, each citing the other without independent verification. This technique generates false credibility by transforming isolated propaganda into an apparent consensus.

A typical case involves one regime-linked outlet publishing a claim, which is then referenced by a think tank or foreign commentator, and cited by another outlet, eventually creating a well-sourced trail. The original fabrication is concealed under layers of interlinked affirmations.

Discussion

Ambiguity and Reality

While legitimation techniques appear coordinated and multifaceted, they do not result from a single doctrine or central command structure. Instead, they operate through dispersed mechanisms, legacy institutions, and adaptive experimentation. This decentralization has strategic benefits but also introduces ambiguities that are important to understand for anyone analyzing or countering these influence operations.

  • Plausible Deniability: Disaggregation allows the state to shield itself from direct attribution. A media outlet in Serbia, an Orthodox NGO in Georgia, or an academic conference in Berlin can all push Kremlin-aligned messaging without explicit coordination. This “soft distance” complicates countermeasures and makes them more difficult to identify.

  • Structural Legacy: Many post-Soviet institutions, such as Rossotrudnichestvo, RT, and RIAC, evolved from different branches of Soviet influence work—ideological, cultural, and intelligence-linked. They retain distinct bureaucratic lineages, and full integration would undermine their institutional cover.

  • Operational Redundancy: Duplication across channels creates resiliency. If one front group is exposed, others continue without disruption. This mimicry of organic complexity prevents adversaries from easily dismantling it.

  • Hybrid Warfare Doctrine: In today’s “non-linear conflict” environment (adjacent to the Gerasimov Doctrine), distributed and self-adaptive operations are more survivable. Influence is meant to feel ambient, not imposed from above.

The result is not a master plan, but a modular ecosystem: a blend of state-run, state-linked, and sympathetic actors operating in parallel, often without formal coordination, but with a shared ideological DNA.

Legitimation ≠ Legitimacy

It is essential to clarify that “legitimation” in this context is not about gaining genuine domestic legitimacy or moral leadership in the democratic sense. It is about engineering perception, especially externally, through borrowed or falsified signals of authenticity.

  • It’s performance, not reality. Many legitimation tactics are not designed to change minds through argument but to mimic credible sources. This allows them to erode consensus.

  • It’s about creating doubt, not converting. The primary audience is often civil society or regulators. Creating “noise” makes it harder for watchdogs, journalists, or policymakers to know what is true. For those who have reason to delay action or deny harm, this environment makes that more palatable for the public..

  • It creates epistemic fatigue. By weaponizing indicators of credibility—flooding discourse with half-truths, dubious experts, or contradictory claims—regimes reduce a person’s ability to distinguish fact from framing.

In this sense, legitimation is not a path to political acceptance but a smokescreen—a way to make contested ideas appear reasonable, grounded, or grassroots.

Not All Influence Is Covert—or Coherent

While many methods cataloged here are subtle or disguised, others are overt—and intentionally so. For example, the Valdai Club hosts prominent Western academics alongside Kremlin officials to create a visible dialogue. This isn’t about hiding influence—it’s about normalizing it. The behaviors, scenes, and signs found in democratic countries appear as a show designed to ease the conscience of those who must deal with the autocratic regime.

Not all initiatives are consistent, however. Some information manipulation campaigns push mutually exclusive narratives (COVID-19 is both a hoax and a U.S. bioweapon). This lack of coherence is not a flaw but a feature. To draw a false conclusion would allow a person to act, but drawing no conclusion creates a state of paralysis.

Third-Country Fronts Obscure Russian Origins

Outsourcing legitimation to actors in Africa, Latin America, and certain parts of Asia has been a staple in both the past and the present. This creates a post-colonial optics inversion: critiques of the West come from the Global South but are often fertilized with Kremlin narratives or resources. This creates additional difficulty in attribution, and legitimacy laundering appears more authentic when information manipulation is localized in tone and origin.

Informational Fog vs. Persuasive Clarity

One of the most counterintuitive features of legitimation is that its goal is not always to be believed—it is often enough to be repeated.

  • A report doesn’t need to be credible—just circulated.
  • An expert doesn’t need credentials—just airtime.
  • A front group doesn’t need to be convincing—just visible.
  • A claim doesn’t need to be true—just repeated.

The purpose is not persuasive clarity but repeat appearances echoing the same message. Through repetition and apparent experts, the appearance of credibility leads an audience to translate it as credible.
