Legitimation

function
Legitimation is the strategic function through which regimes manufacture the appearance of authenticity, consensus, and moral credibility for their narratives and actions.
Author

E. Rosalie Li

Information Epidemiology Lab

Published

February 24, 2026

Legitimation is the strategic function through which regimes manufacture the appearance of authenticity, consensus, and moral credibility for their narratives and actions. Unlike brute force or overt censorship, legitimation cloaks manipulation in the garments of trust—academia, civil society, science, culture, and grassroots activism. It transforms propaganda from an act of coercion into one of persuasion, making ideology seem organic rather than imposed. In essence, legitimation “launders” intent: embedding regime interests within familiar and seemingly independent institutions so that hostile narratives gain a sheen of credibility. This stands in contrast to the Command and Control function of influence (see the Command and Control chapter), which is about synchronization and dominance; legitimation, instead, is about credibility and consent. It leverages cultural soft power, epistemic authority, and moral language to stabilize influence efforts, create echo chambers of support, and undermine critical resistance. By weaponizing reputable platforms and voices, legitimation makes state-sponsored narratives appear normal, popular, or expert-endorsed rather than driven by a regime.

Many of these methods were first institutionalized by the Soviet Union through KGB Active Measures (Directorate A) and the International Department of the Communist Party.(Andrew & Mitrokhin, 2000; Rid, 2020) They have not vanished—they have migrated, adapted, and reappeared across modern authoritarian regimes under new guises, from “multipolarity” to “sovereign democracy.” Today’s authoritarian states build on that legacy with updated technologies and global reach, but the underlying goal remains the same: launder official narratives through seemingly independent channels. This creates an ecosystem of front organizations, sympathetic voices, and pseudo-independent initiatives that together sustain a disinformation architecture. Legitimation functions as the backbone of this architecture, continually supplying each campaign with signs and symbols of credibility and legitimacy.

Legitimation is an operational layer of influence, not merely a rhetorical style. It provides the credibility infrastructure that other influence functions rely on. A regime can flood social media with fake accounts (Obfuscation) or command proxies to amplify messages (Operational Multipliers), but without some veneer of legitimacy, these messages won’t stick. Legitimation efforts inject that veneer: planting stories in respected journals, having sympathetic “experts” echo talking points on news panels, or sponsoring cultural events that align the regime with shared humanistic values. In so doing, legitimation blurs the line between genuine civic discourse and state-driven messaging. As later sections will show, this involves a wide range of tactics—from state-backed activist fronts to scientific mimicry—all converging on the same objective: make the state’s preferred position look and feel credible.

Legitimation operates by mimicry and mirroring. Authoritarian influence agents often mimic the forms of legitimate democratic discourse (protests, petitions, scholarly research, philanthropic grants, awards ceremonies) while inverting their function. A youth festival or peace conference in an open society is a forum for genuine cultural exchange; a similar event funded by an authoritarian state is engineered to echo that state’s worldview. By exploiting the openness of democratic systems—free press, academic freedom, pluralism of civil society—authoritarian actors effectively weaponize legitimate institutions against themselves. This creates complex attribution challenges, as many unwitting intermediaries (journalists, scholars, activists) may genuinely believe they are pursuing independent activities, not realizing they have been co-opted. As a result, responses to legitimation tactics require great care: blunt countermeasures risk harming innocent actors or undermining the very openness that disinformation campaigns exploit.

Discussion

Ambiguity and Reality

While legitimation techniques appear coordinated and multifaceted, they do not result from a single doctrine or central command structure. Instead, they operate through dispersed mechanisms, legacy institutions, and adaptive experimentation. This decentralization has strategic benefits but also introduces ambiguities that are important to understand for anyone analyzing or countering these influence operations.

  • Plausible Deniability: Disaggregation allows the state to shield itself from direct attribution. A media outlet in Serbia, an Orthodox NGO in Georgia, or an academic conference in Berlin can all push Kremlin-aligned messaging without explicit coordination. This “soft distance” complicates countermeasures and makes them more difficult to identify.

  • Structural Legacy: Many post-Soviet institutions, such as Rossotrudnichestvo, RT, and RIAC, evolved from different branches of Soviet influence work—ideological, cultural, and intelligence-linked. They retain distinct bureaucratic lineages, and full integration would undermine their institutional cover.

  • Operational Redundancy: Duplication across channels creates resiliency. If one front group is exposed, others continue without disruption. This mimicry of organic complexity prevents adversaries from easily dismantling it.

  • Hybrid Warfare Doctrine: In today’s “non-linear conflict” environment (adjacent to the Gerasimov Doctrine), distributed and self-adaptive operations are more survivable. Influence is meant to feel ambient, not imposed from above.

The result is not a master plan, but a modular ecosystem: a blend of state-run, state-linked, and sympathetic actors operating in parallel, often without formal coordination, but with a shared ideological DNA.

Legitimation ≠ Legitimacy

It is essential to clarify that “legitimation” in this context is not about gaining genuine domestic legitimacy or moral leadership in the democratic sense. It is about engineering perception, especially externally, through borrowed or falsified signals of authenticity.

  • It’s performance, not reality. Many legitimation tactics are not designed to change minds through argument but to mimic credible sources. This allows them to erode consensus.

  • It’s about creating doubt, not converting. The primary audience is often civil society or regulators. Creating “noise” makes it harder for watchdogs, journalists, or policymakers to know what is true. For those who have reason to delay action or deny harm, this environment makes that more palatable for the public..

  • It creates epistemic fatigue. By weaponizing indicators of credibility—flooding discourse with half-truths, dubious experts, or contradictory claims—regimes reduce a person’s ability to distinguish fact from framing.

In this sense, legitimation is not a path to political acceptance but a smokescreen—a way to make contested ideas appear reasonable, grounded, or grassroots.

Not All Influence Is Covert—or Coherent

While many methods cataloged here are subtle or disguised, others are overt—and intentionally so. For example, the Valdai Club hosts prominent Western academics alongside Kremlin officials to create a visible dialogue. This isn’t about hiding influence—it’s about normalizing it. The behaviors, scenes, and signs found in democratic countries appear as a show designed to ease the conscience of those who must deal with the autocratic regime.

Not all initiatives are consistent, however. Some information manipulation campaigns push mutually exclusive narratives (COVID-19 is both a hoax and a U.S. bioweapon). This lack of coherence is not a flaw but a feature. To draw a false conclusion would allow a person to act, but drawing no conclusion creates a state of paralysis.

Third-Country Fronts Obscure Russian Origins

Outsourcing legitimation to actors in Africa, Latin America, and certain parts of Asia has been a staple in both the past and the present. This creates a post-colonial optics inversion: critiques of the West come from the Global South but are often fertilized with Kremlin narratives or resources. This creates additional difficulty in attribution, and legitimacy laundering appears more authentic when information manipulation is localized in tone and origin.

Informational Fog vs. Persuasive Clarity

One of the most counterintuitive features of legitimation is that its goal is not always to be believed—it is often enough to be repeated.

  • A report doesn’t need to be credible—just circulated.
  • An expert doesn’t need credentials—just airtime.
  • A front group doesn’t need to be convincing—just visible.
  • A claim doesn’t need to be true—just repeated.

The purpose is not persuasive clarity but repeat appearances echoing the same message. Through repetition and apparent experts, the appearance of credibility leads an audience to translate it as credible.
